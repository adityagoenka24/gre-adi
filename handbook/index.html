How to use this handbook

This is not a textbook. It is the last thing you read before a mock, and the last thing you read before the real exam. Nothing here teaches a topic from scratch — every line assumes you have met the idea before and need it back in working memory fast.

Read it in one of three ways, depending on how much time you have.

You have Read Why
90 minutes The whole handbook, front to back, once The full revision pass. Do this the day before a mock.
25 minutes Every Formulas and rules box, skipping Traps and Speed The formula-only pass. Do this the morning of a mock.
10 minutes Section A, plus Section 9 (the 25 costly traps), plus the Appendix The night-before pass. No new learning, only recall.

The one rule for using it well. When a line surprises you, stop and open the full concept note for that topic. A formula you cannot immediately explain is a formula you will misapply under time pressure. Surprise is the signal that you need the note, not the handbook.

Notation used throughout. Mathematics is written in plain text so this reads correctly on paper, on a phone and in print: sqrt(x) is the square root of x, x^2 is x squared, pi is about 3.14, |x| is the absolute value of x, and a/b is a divided by b. Multiplication is written with * where it could be ambiguous, and by juxtaposition otherwise.

Section A — The test itself

Know this cold. These are marks you lose to the format, not to the mathematics.

A.1 Structure and timing

Section Questions Time Average per question
Quant 1 12 21 minutes about 1 minute 45 seconds
Quant 2 15 26 minutes about 1 minute 44 seconds
  • No penalty for a wrong answer. Never leave a blank. A guess is strictly better than nothing.
  • Adaptive between sections, not within. How you do on the first Quant section sets the difficulty of the second. Within a section, difficulty tells you nothing about how you are doing.
  • You may move freely inside a section. Flag a question, finish the section, come back.
  • An on-screen calculator is provided. It is a four-function calculator with a square root. It is slower than your head for small arithmetic and it does not respect order of operations the way you expect. Use it for long division, awkward decimals and square roots, and for nothing else.

A.2 The real time budget

The average is misleading, because the hard questions need more than the average and the easy ones need far less. Work to these targets instead.

Question type Target
Quantitative comparison 60 to 75 seconds
Straightforward multiple choice 60 to 90 seconds
Data interpretation, per question 60 to 90 seconds, plus 40 seconds to read the set first
Hard multi-step problem up to 2 minutes, then move on

Banking 30 seconds on each of six easy questions buys three extra minutes for the hard ones. That trade is the whole game.

The 30-second rule. If you cannot see a route into a problem within 30 seconds, flag it and move on. Every question is worth the same mark. There is no prize for answering them in order.

Two passes, always. First pass: everything you can do at speed. Second pass: the flagged ones.

A.3 The four question formats

Format How you recognize it The rule that matters
Multiple choice, one answer 5 options, oval buttons Exactly one is correct. Back-solving is available.
Multiple choice, one or more 3 to 8 options, square boxes Select every correct one. No partial credit.
Numeric entry A box, or two boxes for a fraction No options, so no elimination and no safety net.
Quantitative comparison The standard four choices Compare; do not compute.

Oval buttons mean pick one. Square boxes mean pick all that apply. Check the shape before you answer. It takes one second and it prevents a full-mark loss.

On multiple-answer questions: there is no partial credit, so two right out of three scores zero. The number of correct options is usually not stated. Walk down the list once, treating each option as its own true-or-false question, and do not stop when you have found two.

On numeric entry: your work has to be right, because nothing on screen will catch you. Budget an extra 15 seconds to sanity-check. Enter a fraction in the two-box form when it is offered, and it need not be reduced. Follow any rounding instruction exactly. Do not type units, commas or symbols. Use the minus sign in the box for a negative answer.

A.4 Quantitative comparison — the method

Roughly a third to a half of every Quant section, so expect somewhere around five or six in the first section and six or seven in the second. The four choices are always the same, in this order:

Quantity A is greater.
Quantity B is greater.
The two quantities are equal.
The relationship cannot be determined from the information given.

The decision rule for the fourth choice. Choose it if and only if you can produce two cases that give different answers. One case with Quantity A greater and one case with the two equal is enough.

When the fourth choice is impossible. If both quantities are pure numbers with no variable in sight, the relationship is fixed, even if you cannot yet see it. The answer can never be the fourth choice.

Operations you may perform on both quantities. They behave exactly like inequalities.

Operation Safe?
Add or subtract the same value Yes
Multiply or divide by the same positive value Yes
Multiply or divide by a negative value No — it reverses the relationship
Multiply or divide by an expression of unknown sign No
Square both quantities Only when both are known to be non-negative

The FROZEN test values. When a variable is unrestricted, test these before concluding anything:

F  Fractions        (try 0.5)
R  Repeats          (make the variables equal)
O  One
Z  Zero
E  Extremes         (very large, very small)
N  Negatives

If two of them disagree, the answer is the fourth choice. If they all agree, the answer is probably one of the first three — but test at least three values before you commit.

Speed on QC. Strip away whatever is common to both quantities and compare what is left; the remainder is usually trivial. Estimate rather than compute whenever the gap is wide. If the two quantities look designed to be equal, suspect "equal" and then test one edge case to confirm.

A.5 Test-day rules that are not mathematics

  • Read the last line again before you answer. Solving for x and then answering with x when the question asked for 2x is the single most common avoidable error on the test.
  • Underline what is asked before you start solving.
  • Write down every intermediate value. Re-deriving a number you already had is pure waste, and mental algebra under time pressure produces sign errors.
  • Sanity-check the shape of the answer. A negative length, a probability above 1, a percentage over 100, an average outside the range of the data: each of these means you have made an error.
  • Do not assume a figure is drawn to scale unless the question says so. In quantitative comparison especially, many are deliberately not.
  • A hard question means nothing. Difficulty within a section is not feedback. Move on.

Section 1 — Arithmetic

1.1 Percentages

Every percent question is really the question percent of what**. Errors here are almost never arithmetic; they are a wrong choice of base.

Formulas and rules

Need Method
x percent of y (x/100) * y
Find the part multiply: 15 percent of 80 is 0.15 * 80
Find the whole divide: 12 is 15 percent of what, so 12 / 0.15
Find the percent (part / whole) * 100
Percent change (New - Old) / Old * 100 — always divide by the original
Increase by x percent multiply by 1 + x/100
Decrease by x percent multiply by 1 - x/100
Successive changes multiply the multipliers: 1.40 * 0.80 * 0.90
Reverse a change divide by the multiplier, do not subtract the percent

Turn every percent into a multiplier, then multiply. One line instead of three calculations.

The six traps

Trap Wrong Right
New value used as the base 50 to 60 is 10/60 = 16.7 percent 50 to 60 is 10/50 = 20 percent
Adding successive percents up 30 then down 30 is 0 1.30 * 0.70 = 0.91, a 9 percent loss
"More than" read as "of" A is 40 percent more than B, so A = 0.40B A = 1.40B
Percent against percentage points 5 percent to 7 percent is "a 2 percent rise" 2 percentage points, but a 40 percent rise
Percent of a percent 20 percent of 30 percent is 10 percent 0.20 * 0.30 = 6 percent
Equal fall and rise assumed to cancel down 20 then up 20 returns to the start 0.80 * 1.20 = 0.96, still 4 percent down

Order does not matter in a multiplier chain: 0.8 * 1.2 and 1.2 * 0.8 both give 0.96. A discount then a tax equals a tax then a discount, so never recompute it.

Speed

  • Pick 100. When the problem is all percents and no amounts, set the start value to 100.
  • Split the percent. 15 percent of 240 is 10 percent plus 5 percent, so 24 + 12 = 36.
  • Swap the terms. x percent of y equals y percent of x. So 8 percent of 25 is 25 percent of 8, or 2.
  • One percent, then scale. 17 percent of 350: one percent is 3.5, so 3.5 * 17 = 59.5.
  • Percent as a fraction. 12.5 percent is 1/8, so dividing by 8 beats multiplying by 0.125.
  • Predict the direction first. Decide whether the answer is bigger or smaller than the start. Half of all trap answers fail that check instantly.

1.2 Fractions and decimals

Formulas and rules

Operation Rule
a/b + c/d (ad + bc) / bd
a/b - c/d (ad - bc) / bd
a/b * c/d ac / bd
a/b divided by c/d a/b * d/c — flip the second one
(a/b)^n a^n / b^n
Compare a/b with c/d cross-multiply: ad against bc, valid when b and d are positive
Mixed to improper a b/c = (ac + b)/c

Size intuition

  • Same numerator, bigger denominator gives a smaller fraction: 3/7 < 3/5.
  • Same denominator, bigger numerator gives a bigger fraction: 5/7 > 3/7.
  • Adding the same positive k to the top and bottom of a proper fraction moves it toward 1, so it gets bigger: 2/3 < 3/4 < 4/5.
  • For an improper fraction, adding k to both moves it down toward 1: 5/3 > 6/4 > 7/5.

Terminating or recurring. In lowest terms, a fraction terminates if and only if the denominator's prime factorization contains only 2s and 5s. 7/40 = 7/(2^3 * 5) terminates at 0.175; 5/12 = 5/(2^2 * 3) recurs as 0.41666...

Decimals. Multiplying: multiply as integers, then the answer carries as many decimal places as the two factors combined, so 0.03 * 0.4 gives 12 with three places, or 0.012. Dividing: shift both points right until the divisor is a whole number. And 0.999... equals 1 exactly.

Traps

  • Adding across. 1/2 + 1/3 is not 2/5.
  • Not reducing before judging a denominator. 9/24 looks like it has a 3 on the bottom; reduced to 3/8 it does not, so it terminates.
  • Flipping the wrong fraction when dividing. Flip the divisor.
  • Squaring a fraction between 0 and 1 makes it smaller. (1/2)^2 = 1/4. The square root of a number between 0 and 1 is larger than the number.
  • Negative fractions reverse intuition. -1/2 > -3/4, because it is closer to zero.
  • Decimal place miscount. 0.2 * 0.3 is 0.06, not 0.6.

Speed

  • Stay in fractions until the final step, especially on numeric entry, which accepts fractions.
  • Cancel before multiplying. (14/25) * (75/28): cancel 14 against 28 and 75 against 25 to get 3/2.
  • Cross-multiply to compare. It beats converting to decimals every time.
  • Benchmark against 1/2 and 1 for fast triage.
  • Recurring decimals worth knowing: 1/3 = 0.333, 1/6 = 0.1666, 1/7 = 0.142857 repeating, 1/9 = 0.111, 1/11 = 0.0909.

1.3 Ratios and proportions

A ratio is not an amount. Write ak and bk, then use the extra fact in the stem to find k.

Formulas and rules

Concept Rule
Part to whole from a : b first part is a/(a+b) of the total
Introducing the multiplier a : b means the amounts are ak and bk
Sum of parts a + b + c divides the total; if it does not divide cleanly, re-read the stem
Direct proportion y = kx; one rises, the other rises
Inverse proportion xy = k; one rises, the other falls
Chaining A:B with B:C scale so the shared term matches
Similar figures, side ratio k lengths k, areas k^2, volumes k^3
Comparing a : b with c : d cross-multiply, as with fractions

Chaining worked. A:B = 2:3 and B:C = 4:5. B is 3 then 4, so scale to 12: A:B = 8:12 and B:C = 12:15, giving A:B:C = 8:12:15.

The six traps

Trap Wrong Right
Part-to-part read as part-to-whole boys : girls = 2:3, so boys are 2/3 of the class boys are 2/5
Changing both parts when only one moved (2k+10)/(3k+10) = 4/3 (2k+10)/(3k) = 4/3
Chaining without matching the shared term A:B:C = 2:3:5 8:12:15
Reading the ratio as the amounts 3:5 means 3 boys and 5 girls it means 3k and 5k
Direct used where inverse belongs 12 workers take 15 * 12/8 days 12 workers take 15 * 8/12 = 10 days
Forgetting to square or cube sides 2:3, so areas 2:3 areas 4:9, volumes 8:27

Speed

  • Always name k, even on easy problems. It turns a ratio problem into an equation problem.
  • Reduce the ratio before introducing k. 6:10:16 and 3:5:8 give the same shares with half the arithmetic.
  • Predict the direction. "More workers, fewer days" kills the direct-against-inverse trap with no algebra at all.
  • Track the quantity that does not change in any mixture or change problem — usually the pure solute, or the group nobody joined or left.
  • Re-read the final sentence. Ratio problems routinely ask for the amount added, or for B when you solved for k.

1.4 Exponents and roots

Formulas and rules

Rule Statement
Product a^m * a^n = a^(m+n)
Quotient a^m / a^n = a^(m-n)
Power of a power (a^m)^n = a^(mn)
Power of a product (ab)^n = a^n b^n
Power of a quotient (a/b)^n = a^n / b^n
Zero exponent a^0 = 1 for a not equal to 0
Negative exponent a^(-n) = 1/a^n, for a not equal to 0
Fractional exponent a^(m/n) is the n-th root of a^m
Root as a power sqrt(a) = a^(1/2), cube root of a is a^(1/3)

What the rules do not say

  • a^m + a^n does not become a^(m+n). Addition has no exponent rule. Factor instead: 2^5 + 2^6 = 2^5(1 + 2) = 3 * 2^5.
  • (a + b)^2 is not a^2 + b^2. It is a^2 + 2ab + b^2.
  • sqrt(a^2 + b^2) is not a + b.

Signs. (-2)^4 = 16 (even power, positive). (-2)^3 = -8 (odd power, negative). But -2^4 = -16, because the minus sits outside and only the 2 is raised.

Roots. The symbol sqrt(x) denotes the non-negative root, so sqrt(25) = 5 and never -5. But the equation x^2 = 25 has two solutions, x = 5 and x = -5. This distinction is tested constantly. Also sqrt(x^2) = |x|, not x.

Simplifying. Pull out perfect squares: sqrt(72) = sqrt(36 * 2) = 6 sqrt(2). Rationalizing. 1/sqrt(3) = sqrt(3)/3. For 1/(2 + sqrt(3)), multiply above and below by the conjugate 2 - sqrt(3) to get 2 - sqrt(3).

Traps

  • (-2)^2 against -2^2. With brackets, 4. Without, -4.
  • x^2 = 16 gives x = 4 only. No: x = 4 or x = -4. Missing the negative root is the number-one exponent error on quantitative comparison.
  • Adding exponents across different bases. 2^3 * 3^3 is 6^3, not 6^6.
  • Fractional bases shrink. (1/2)^5 is smaller than (1/2)^2.
  • a^(1/2) read as half of a. It is the square root.
  • A negative exponent read as a negative number. 2^(-3) = 1/8.

Speed

  • Prime-factorize everything. Any exponent equation becomes trivial once every base is a prime power.
  • Factor, do not expand, whenever you see a sum of powers with the same base.
  • To compare a^m with b^n, force a common base or a common exponent — whichever is easier.
  • Bracket roots. sqrt(50) lies between 7 and 8 and close to 7, so about 7.07.
  • Units-digit cycles repeat with period at most 4: 2 gives 2,4,8,6; 3 gives 3,9,7,1; 7 gives 7,9,3,1; 8 gives 8,4,2,6. The digits 0, 1, 5 and 6 never change.

1.5 Rates, work and unit conversion

Formulas and rules

Concept Formula
Distance Speed * Time
Work done Rate * Time
Working together add the rates: R1 + R2
Fill against drain subtract: R1 - R2
Average speed Total distance / Total time — always
Equal distances at speeds a and b 2ab/(a + b)
Equal times at speeds a and b (a + b)/2
Same direction relative speed is the difference
Opposite directions relative speed is the sum
Unit conversion multiply by a fraction equal to 1, desired/given

Average speed is never the average of the speeds unless the times are equal.

Conversions. 1 hour is 3600 seconds and 1 kilometer is 1000 meters. To convert kilometers per hour to meters per second, multiply by 5/18; to go back, multiply by 18/5. One mile is about 1.6 kilometers and 1 kilogram is about 2.2 pounds, though the GRE normally supplies any non-metric factor.

Traps

  • Averaging the speeds on an equal-distance round trip. That is the planted wrong answer.
  • Not converting units first. Speed in kilometers per hour with time in minutes needs a conversion before you multiply.
  • Adding times instead of rates. Two people taking 6 hours and 12 hours do not together take 18 hours, nor 9 by averaging. They take 4. Add their rates.
  • Forgetting that a leak subtracts. Rates add only when both agents push the same way.
  • "50 percent faster" is not "half the time". Rate times 1.5 means time times 2/3.
  • Rounding mid-problem. 20/3 hours is exact; 6.67 invites error.

Speed

  • Use the least common multiple as the total work. For workers taking 6 and 12 hours, set the job at 12 units: A does 2 units an hour, B does 1, together 3, so 12/3 = 4 hours. No fractions at all.
  • Chain the units as a product of fractions and cancel diagonally. If the leftover units are right, the arithmetic is right.
  • Memorize * 5/18 for kilometers per hour into meters per second.
  • For "when do they meet", add the speeds and divide the gap by that sum.
  • Predict whether the answer sits nearer the faster or the slower value. This catches trap answers instantly.

1.6 Remainders and cyclicity

Formulas and rules

Concept Rule
Division statement N = dq + r with 0 <= r < d
Remainder range always less than the divisor; 0 is allowed and means divisible
Sum the remainder of a + b is the remainder of (remainder of a plus remainder of b)
Product the remainder of a * b is the remainder of (remainder of a times remainder of b)
Power reduce the base to its remainder first, then use the cycle
Last digit the remainder on division by 10
Reading the stem "leaves remainder 3 when divided by 7" means N = 7k + 3

Units-digit cycles for powers

Units digit of the base Cycle Length
0, 1, 5, 6 itself 1
4 4, 6 2
9 9, 1 2
2 2, 4, 8, 6 4
3 3, 9, 7, 1 4
7 7, 9, 3, 1 4
8 8, 4, 2, 6 4

For a cycle of length 4, divide the exponent by 4 and read the remainder. A remainder of 0 means use the fourth entry, not the first.

Traps

  • Treating remainder 0 as invalid. Zero is a perfectly good remainder.
  • Leaving a remainder at or above the divisor. A remainder of 9 on division by 7 reduces to 2.
  • Adding remainders without re-reducing. 4 plus 5 against a divisor of 7 gives 9, which reduces to 2.
  • Negative remainders. The GRE uses non-negative remainders: -3 divided by 5 leaves 2.
  • Off-by-one on cycles. For 2^12, the exponent leaves remainder 0 on division by 4, so take the fourth entry, 6.
  • Assuming a unique answer. "n leaves remainder 3 on division by 7" describes infinitely many n, so on quantitative comparison test at least two of them.

Speed

  • Write N = dq + r immediately. Nearly every remainder word problem becomes algebra once you do.
  • Pick the smallest valid number, then one more to confirm.
  • Reduce before you multiply. For 97 * 98 against a divisor of 5, use 2 * 3 = 6, which leaves 1.
  • Same remainder for two divisors points to the least common multiple plus r.
  • Last two digits work the same way, against a divisor of 100.

1.7 Absolute value

Read |x| as a distance from zero, and |a - b| as the distance between a and b.

Formulas and rules

Statement Meaning
\|x\| = x when x >= 0, -x when x < 0 and -x is positive in that second case
\|x\| >= 0 always
\|-x\| = \|x\|

The general pattern. |x - c| < r says x is within r of c, which is the interval from c - r to c + r. So |x - 3| < 5 gives -2 < x < 8.

Traps

  • Taking only the positive case. |x| = 5 has two answers.
  • Treating |x| > k like |x| < k. Less-than is one interval; greater-than is two rays.
  • Distributing the bars over a sum. With a = 3 and b = -3, |a + b| = 0 but |a| + |b| = 6.
  • Assuming sqrt(x^2) = x. It is |x|.
  • Skipping the extraneous-solution check when the variable also appears outside the bars, as in |x - 2| = 3x. Solve both cases, substitute back, discard whatever fails.
  • Forgetting to flip an inequality when you multiply or divide by a negative.

Speed

  • Read it as distance. |x - 7| = 2 means x is 2 away from 7, so x = 5 or x = 9. No algebra.
  • Sketch the number line. Mark the center and the radius; the answer is inside or outside.
  • On quantitative comparison, test a negative whenever a variable inside bars is unrestricted. That is where the trap lives.
  • |a| = |b| means a = b or a = -b. Two cases, always.
  • The least value of |x - a| + |x - b| is |a - b|, reached for any x between a and b.

Place value. 10^3 is a thousand, 10^6 a million, 10^9 a billion, 10^(-2) a hundredth and 10^(-3) a thousandth.

  • A negative exponent read as a negative number. 10^(-4) = 0.0001, positive and small.
  • Adding coefficients without matching exponents. 3 * 10^5 + 2 * 10^3 is neither 5 * 10^8 nor 5 * 10^5.

  • Miscounting decimal places. 0.0034 is 3.4 * 10^(-3). Count from the point to the first non-zero digit.
  • Assuming more digits means bigger. 1.9 * 10^7 is far smaller than 2.0 * 10^9.

Speed

  • Split every number into coefficient and power, then handle the two parts separately.
  • For "approximately" questions, round the coefficient to one digit and just track the exponent.
  • For "how many times greater", divide — the answer falls out as a power of 10.
  • Check the sign of the exponent: above 1 is positive, between 0 and 1 is negative.

1.10 Estimation and approximation

When to estimate

Situation Approach

"Approximately" in the stem estimation is intended — but check the spread
Percent of a large number use 10 percent and scale

Fractions round to 1/2, 1/3 or 3/4

Controlling the direction of the error. This is the part most students miss. Rounding the numerator up and the denominator down makes your estimate too big twice over. Rounding both the same way lets the errors partly cancel. For 487/103, true value 4.73, rounding to 500/100 = 5 overestimates twice, while 500/110 = 4.55 or 490/105 = 4.67 land much closer.

Root approximation. sqrt(N) sits between the two nearest perfect squares, roughly in proportion. For sqrt(60): between 7 and 8, and 60 is 11 above 49 across a gap of 15, so about 7.7. The true value is 7.746.

Traps

  • Estimating when an exact value is wanted. Check the answer spread first.
  • Compounding the error in a product. In a quotient, rounding both parts the same way makes the two errors cancel; in a product it makes them pile up. Rounding 4.6 up to 5 and 2.4 up to 3 gives 15 against a true 11.04, a 36 percent miss.
  • Over-rounding percentages. Treating 19.8 percent as 20 percent is fine; treating 17 percent as 20 percent usually is not.
  • Rounding before simplifying. Cancel common factors first; the exact answer often becomes easy.
  • Estimating on quantitative comparison when the quantities are close. If your estimates come out nearly equal, you must compute exactly — an estimate cannot separate "A is greater" from "equal".

Speed

  • Read the answer choices before calculating. Their spread tells you the precision you need.
  • Ten percent, then scale. 35 percent of 480 is 48 * 3.5 = 168.
  • Double and halve. 25 * 36 = 50 * 18 = 100 * 9 = 900.
  • Percent as a fraction. 12.5 percent is 1/8, 16.67 percent is 1/6, 37.5 percent is 3/8.
  • Check the order of magnitude last. Even with shaky digits, getting 10^3 against 10^4 right usually identifies the answer.

The test in practice. Is 191 prime? sqrt(191) is about 13.8, so test only 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13. Not even; digit sum 11 is not a multiple of 3; does not end in 0 or 5; and 7, 11, 13 all leave a remainder. 191 is prime.

Speed

  • Memorize the primes to 50. It pays for itself many times over.
  • Every prime above 3 is one either side of a multiple of 6. Useful for elimination, though not every such number is prime.

The tests

The 11 test in detail. Add the digits in the odd positions from the right and subtract the digits in the even positions. For 82,918: (8 + 9 + 8) - (2 + 1) = 22, a multiple of 11, so 82,918 is divisible by 11.

Splitting a composite divisor — the essential caution. Break it into coprime factors. 12 = 3 * 4 is coprime, so testing 3 and 4 is valid. 12 = 2 * 6 is not coprime, so testing 2 and 6 is not enough: 18 passes both and is not a multiple of 12.

Divisibility of expressions. If d divides a and d divides b, then d divides a + b, a - b, and ka for any integer k.

Traps

  • Testing 12 as 2 and 6. The factors must be coprime; use 3 and 4.
  • Using the 2 rule for 4. Divisibility by 4 needs the last two digits.
  • Using a digit sum for 4 or 8. The digit sum works only for 3 and 9.
  • Assuming a divisible sum means divisible terms. 7 divides 3 + 4 and divides neither.
  • Zero. 0 is divisible by every non-zero integer, and it is even.
  • Sign. -12 is divisible by 3. Divisibility is about a zero remainder, and sign does not affect it.

Speed

  • Digit sum first. It settles 3, 9 and, with the 2 test, 6 in one glance.
  • Prime-factorize the divisor and check each prime power. For 72, check the last three digits for 8 and the digit sum for 9.
  • In "must be divisible by" questions, factor the given expression rather than plugging in numbers.

2.4 Greatest common divisor and least common multiple

Abbreviated GCD and LCM below for compactness.

Formulas and rules

Concept Rule
GCD from the factorizations the lowest power of each shared prime
LCM from the factorizations the highest power of every prime present
The identity GCD(a,b) * LCM(a,b) = a * b — two numbers only
Coprime GCD is 1, and then the LCM is a * b
GCD range 1 <= GCD <= the smaller of a and b
LCM range the larger of a and b <= LCM <= a * b
GCD divides LCM always

The mechanic. For 84 and 120: 84 = 2^2 * 3 * 7 and 120 = 2^3 * 3 * 5. The GCD takes the lowest power of the shared primes 2 and 3, giving 2^2 * 3 = 12. The LCM takes the highest power of every prime, giving 2^3 * 3 * 5 * 7 = 840. Check: 12 * 840 = 10,080 and 84 * 120 = 10,080.

Traps

  • Swapping the two. The GCD is small, at most the smaller number; the LCM is large, at least the larger. If your GCD exceeds either number, you computed the LCM.
  • Using the highest power for the GCD. It takes the lowest power, and only of shared primes.
  • Applying the identity to three numbers. It fails: for 2, 4 and 8 the GCD is 2 and the LCM is 8, but the product of the three is 64.
  • "Divisible by 4 and by 6" read as "divisible by 24". It means divisible by 12, their LCM.
  • Forgetting a prime that appears in only one number when building the LCM.
  • Word-problem misread. "The same number of items in every group" is a GCD question; "the smallest length both can be cut into" is an LCM question.

Speed

  • Lay the primes down a column with exponents across. The GCD is the row minimum, the LCM the row maximum.
  • Repeated remainders for two large numbers. Replace the larger number by the remainder it leaves on division by the smaller, and repeat until the remainder is 0; the last non-zero value is the GCD. For 252 and 105: 252 leaves 42, then 105 leaves 21, then 42 leaves 0, so the GCD is 21.
  • If one number divides the other, the GCD is the smaller and the LCM is the larger. Instant.
  • Consecutive integers are always coprime, so the GCD is 1 and the LCM is the product.

Product divisibility. Two consecutive integers give a multiple of 2! = 2; three give a multiple of 3! = 6; four give a multiple of 4! = 24. So n(n+1)(n+2) is a multiple of 6 for every integer n.

Sum parity. The sum of an odd number of consecutive integers is divisible by that count — three consecutive integers sum to three times the middle one. The sum of an even number of consecutive integers is never divisible by the count.

Setting up. For three consecutive integers, n-1, n, n+1 beats n, n+1, n+2, because the sum collapses to 3n immediately.

Traps

  • Assuming consecutive means positive. -3, -2, -1, 0, 1 are consecutive integers.
  • Spacing for consecutive even or odd integers. The gap is 2. Writing n, n+1, n+2 for consecutive odd integers is the standard error.
  • Count off by one. From 7 to 19 inclusive there are 19 - 7 + 1 = 13 integers.
  • Expecting the average to be a member. The average of 4, 5, 6, 7 is 5.5.
  • Assuming the sum is always divisible by the count. True for odd counts only.

Speed

  • Center the variable on an odd-sized set and the sum collapses at once.
  • Sum = count * average, with average = (first + last)/2. The most reusable formula in the topic, and it works for any evenly spaced set, not just consecutive integers.
  • For "must be divisible by" questions, count the consecutive terms in the product and apply the k! rule.
  • Test n = 0, n = 1 and n = 2 on any must-be-true statement.

The two facts students forget: odd + odd = even, and even * anything = even. The only way to get an odd product is for every factor to be odd.

Division has no parity rule. 8/4 = 2, 6/2 = 3, 8/16 = 0.5. Never reason about parity through division.

Special cases. 0 is even, because it is 2 * 0. Negative integers have parity too: -6 is even and -7 is odd. Consecutive integers alternate parity.

Traps

  • odd + odd = odd. It is even. The most common parity slip on the test.
  • Forgetting 0 is even. "How many even integers lie between -3 and 3" must count -2, 0 and 2.
  • Applying parity to division. It does not apply.
  • Assuming a variable is an integer. Parity is defined only for integers; if the stem does not say integer, parity reasoning may not apply at all.
  • even^0. 2^0 = 1, which is odd. The rule needs n >= 1.
  • Reading "not even" as "positive odd". Negative odd integers exist.

Speed

  • Substitute 2 for even and 3 for odd, then test 0 and 1 separately as edge cases.
  • Throw away every even coefficient — it forces its term even, so it cannot change the parity of a sum.
  • n(n+1) is always even. This identity resolves many must-be-true questions instantly.
  • On quantitative comparison, test both parities of any unrestricted integer variable.

Counting with digit constraints — the slot method. Draw one blank per digit, count the allowed choices for each, multiply, and handle the leading slot first because it excludes 0. How many three-digit numbers have all distinct digits? 9 * 9 * 8 = 648.

  • Writing ab for a two-digit number. That means a times b. You need 10a + b.

  • Expand immediately to 10a + b. Never work with the digits informally.

  • Units-digit questions need only the units digits. Discard everything else.
  • For divisibility by 9, use the digit sum rather than dividing.

Closure. The integers are closed under addition, subtraction and multiplication but not division. The rationals are closed under all four, with a non-zero divisor. Zero and one. 0 is an integer, is even, is neither positive nor negative, and is a multiple of every integer. 0/5 = 0 is fine; 5/0 is undefined. 1 is neither prime nor composite, and 1^n = 1.

If 0 < x < 1:   x^2  <  x  <  sqrt(x)  <  1/x

Traps

  • "Number" does not mean "integer". Unless the stem says integer, x could be 2.5 or -1/3.
  • Assuming positive. "x is a number" allows negatives and zero.
  • Assuming irrational times irrational is irrational. sqrt(2) * sqrt(2) = 2.
  • Assuming every non-terminating decimal is irrational. 0.333... is 1/3. Irrational means non-terminating and non-repeating.
  • Assuming x^2 > x. False for 0 < x < 1, and for x = 0 and x = 1.
  • Reciprocal ordering. For positives, reciprocals reverse the order: 2 < 3 but 1/2 > 1/3.

Speed

  • Run the FROZEN check before answering any must-be-true question. If a fraction, zero or a negative breaks the statement, it is not must-be-true.
  • Units digits eliminate perfect-square candidates in one glance.
  • An odd count of factors means a perfect square — a common disguised hint.

When a stem gives you a + b and ab rather than a and b, it is an identity question. You will almost never need a and b separately.

Order of operations. Brackets, exponents, multiply and divide left to right, then add and subtract left to right. So 2 * 3^2 = 18, not 36.

Like terms combine; unlike terms do not. 3x^2 + 5x^2 = 8x^2, but 3x^2 + 5x stays as it is.

Complex fractions. Multiply the top and the bottom by the least common multiple of the inner denominators.

Traps

  • (a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2. The 2ab is missing. This is the single most common algebra error on the test.
  • (a - b)^2 = a^2 - b^2. No. The correct expansion is a^2 - 2ab + b^2; a^2 - b^2 is the difference of squares, which factors as (a + b)(a - b).
  • Distributing a negative to only the first term. -(x - 3) = -x + 3.
  • Canceling across a plus sign. (x + 3)/3 is not x. You may cancel common factors, never terms.
  • sqrt(a^2 + b^2) = a + b. False.
  • Dividing by a variable that might be 0. From x^2 = 3x, factoring gives x(x - 3) = 0, so x = 0 or x = 3. Dividing by x loses the root x = 0.

Speed

  • Look for a^2 - b^2 in any numeric subtraction of squares. It turns heavy arithmetic into a two-step multiplication.
  • Factor before you expand. GRE answer choices are usually in factored form.
  • For any x + 1/x problem, square both sides. That single move solves nearly all of them.
  • Check an identity by substituting x = 2 if you are unsure.

3.2 Factoring

The checklist, in this order

  1. Common factor first. Always. 3x^2 + 6x = 3x(x + 2).
  2. Two terms? Look for a difference of squares, or a sum or difference of cubes.
  3. Three terms? Try the x^2 + (p+q)x + pq pattern, or a perfect-square trinomial.
  4. Four terms? Group in pairs.
Pattern Factored form
a^2 - b^2 (a + b)(a - b)
a^2 + 2ab + b^2 (a + b)^2
a^2 - 2ab + b^2 (a - b)^2
a^3 + b^3 (a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)
a^3 - b^3 (a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2)
x^2 + (p+q)x + pq (x + p)(x + q)

a^2 + b^2 does not factor over the real numbers.

A leading coefficient. For ax^2 + bx + c, find two numbers whose product is ac and whose sum is b, then split the middle term. For 6x^2 + 11x + 3: ac = 18, and 9 and 2 multiply to 18 and sum to 11, so 6x^2 + 9x + 2x + 3 = 3x(2x + 3) + (2x + 3) = (3x + 1)(2x + 3).

Grouping. xy + 3x + 2y + 6 = x(y + 3) + 2(y + 3) = (x + 2)(y + 3).

The zero-product property. AB = 0 means A = 0 or B = 0. This is why you factor rather than divide.

Traps

  • Forgetting the common factor. 2x^3 - 18x written as (x - 3)(x + 3) has lost the 2x.
  • Dividing instead of factoring. From x^2 = 5x, dividing by x loses x = 0.
  • Trying to factor a^2 + b^2.
  • Sign errors in the trinomial split. Expand to check; it costs five seconds.
  • Canceling terms. In (x^2 - 4)/(x + 3) nothing cancels, because x + 3 is not a factor of the numerator.
  • Ignoring domain restrictions. After canceling (x + 2), the original expression is still undefined at x = -2.

Speed

  • Any (something)^2 - (something)^2 factors instantly, even with variables inside: (x+1)^2 - 9 = (x + 4)(x - 2).
  • If the choices are factored, expand one and compare.
  • Sign shortcut for x^2 + bx + c: when c is positive, both factors share the sign of b; when c is negative, the factors have opposite signs and the larger carries the sign of b.
  • Check any factorization at x = 1. Both forms must agree.

  • Multiplying both sides by an expression containing the variable without noting it could be zero. That can create or destroy solutions.
  • Answering the wrong quantity. The stem may want 2x + 1, not x.
  • Assuming a solution exists. Some questions are testing whether you notice "no solution".

Speed

  • Clear fractions first. Integers are always faster than fractions.
  • Move the smaller variable term to avoid negatives.
  • Back-solve from the choices when the algebra is ugly; start with the middle value so one test tells you which way to move.
  • Check by substitution on anything that took over a minute. Ten seconds, and it catches sign errors.

3.4 Systems of equations

Methods

Method Procedure
Substitution isolate one variable, substitute into the other equation
Elimination scale one or both equations so a variable cancels on adding or subtracting
The shortcut add or subtract the equations as they stand and see what emerges

How many solutions — the geometric reading

Situation The lines Solutions
Different slopes cross once exactly one
Same slope, different intercept parallel none
Same slope, same intercept identical infinitely many

Algebraically, for a1 x + b1 y = c1 and a2 x + b2 y = c2: one solution when a1/a2 differs from b1/b2; no solution when a1/a2 = b1/b2 but neither equals c1/c2; infinitely many when a1/a2 = b1/b2 = c1/c2.

Three variables need three independent equations. Eliminate one variable twice to get down to a two-by-two system.

Traps

  • Solving for both variables when only the sum is wanted. Forty wasted seconds.
  • Sign errors in elimination. Subtracting an equation changes the sign of every term in it.
  • Confusing "none" with "infinitely many". Same slope and a different intercept gives none; same slope and the same intercept gives infinitely many.
  • Assuming two equations always pin down two unknowns. Not if one is a multiple of the other.
  • Answering x when the question asked for y, or for xy, or for x/y.

Speed

  • Ask what is actually wanted before you solve. If it is x + y or x - y, try adding or subtracting the raw equations.
  • Symmetric coefficients are a signal. For ax + by = m and bx + ay = n, adding gives (a+b)(x+y) = m+n and subtracting gives (a-b)(x-y) = m-n. Both in one step.
  • Elimination beats substitution when no coefficient is 1.
  • Back-substitute into the equation you did not use. A free check.
  • For "no solution" questions, compare the coefficient ratios rather than solving.

3.5 Inequalities

Formulas and rules

Operation Effect on the sign
Add or subtract anything unchanged
Multiply or divide by a positive unchanged
Multiply or divide by a negative reversed
Take reciprocals of two positives reversed: 2 < 3 but 1/2 > 1/3
Square both sides safe only when both sides are non-negative

Compound inequalities. a < x < b means both conditions, an interval. x < a or x > b means either, two rays. Operate on all three parts at once: from -3 < 2x + 1 < 7, subtract 1 throughout and divide by 2, giving -2 < x < 3.

Combining inequalities. You may add two that point the same way: from a > b and c > d you get a + c > b + d. You may not subtract them. You may multiply only when every quantity is positive.

Quadratic inequalities — the critical-point method. For x^2 - 5x + 6 > 0, factor to (x-2)(x-3) > 0. The critical points are 2 and 3. Test one value in each region: below 2 the product is positive and works; between 2 and 3 it is negative and fails; above 3 it is positive and works. So the answer is x < 2 or x > 3. For < 0 the answer is the middle region, 2 < x < 3.

Traps

  • Forgetting to flip when dividing by a negative. The most common inequality error, full stop.
  • Dividing by a variable of unknown sign. From ax > a you cannot divide by a.
  • Subtracting inequalities. Flip the second and add instead.
  • x^2 < 9 giving x < 3. It gives -3 < x < 3.
  • Squaring carelessly. -5 < 2 is true; 25 < 4 is false.
  • Treating strict endpoints as included. Watch < against <=.

Speed

  • For range questions, test the four corner combinations of the extremes and take the widest result. It works for addition, subtraction and multiplication — and for division only when the divisor's range excludes 0, since a divisor straddling 0 makes the quotient unbounded.
  • Draw the number line and shade. It prevents every and-or mix-up.
  • On quantitative comparison, hunt for a counterexample rather than trying to prove equality.
  • Keep everything positive by moving terms instead of multiplying by negatives. That sidesteps the flip rule entirely.

Sum and product in practice. For 2x^2 - 10x + 8 = 0, the sum of the roots is 10/2 = 5 and the product is 8/2 = 4. The roots are 1 and 4, which confirms both.

Completing the square. x^2 + 6x + 5 = 0 becomes (x + 3)^2 - 9 + 5 = 0, so (x + 3)^2 = 4, giving x = -1 or x = -5.

Quadratic in disguise. x^4 - 5x^2 + 4 = 0 is quadratic in u = x^2: u^2 - 5u + 4 = 0 gives u = 1 or u = 4, so x = +/-1 or x = +/-2. Four solutions.

Traps

  • Missing the negative root. From x^2 = 49, x is 7 or -7. When the GRE hands you x^2 = k, it is usually testing exactly this.
  • Sum of roots written as b/a. It is -b/a. The minus sign is forgotten constantly.
  • Dividing by x and losing the root x = 0.
  • Assuming two roots always exist. With D < 0 there are none over the reals.
  • Forgetting to set the equation to zero before factoring. x^2 = 5x + 24 must become x^2 - 5x - 24 = 0.
  • Extraneous roots. After squaring both sides of an equation with a square root in it, always substitute back; some roots will be invalid.

Speed

  • Try factoring first. GRE quadratics are built to factor most of the time.
  • When the question asks for the sum or the product of the roots, use -b/a and c/a and never solve for the roots individually.
  • Discriminant questions never need the roots. Just compute b^2 - 4ac.
  • Recognize the perfect-square trinomials x^2 + 2kx + k^2 and x^2 - 2kx + k^2.
  • Back-solve from the choices when a quadratic looks ugly.

3.7 Functions

Formulas and rules

Concept Meaning
f(x) the output for input x — a machine, not a product
f(3) substitute 3 for every x
f(a + 1) substitute (a + 1) for every x, keeping the brackets
Domain all allowed inputs
Range all possible outputs

Vertical line test a graph is a function if no vertical line meets it twice

The only two domain restrictions the GRE tests: a denominator cannot be zero, and the expression under an even root cannot be negative. For f(x) = sqrt(x - 3)/(x - 5), the domain is x >= 3 with x not equal to 5.

Composites do not commute. f(g(x)) is usually different from g(f(x)).

Strange-symbol functions. The test often invents an operation, such as a # b = a^2 - 2b. These are ordinary functions in unusual clothing. Substitute exactly as defined and do not overthink it.

Traps

  • Reading f(x) as f times x.
  • f(a + b) = f(a) + f(b). Almost never true. For f(x) = x^2, f(3) = 9 but f(1) + f(2) = 5.
  • Order in composites. f(g(x)) applies the inner function first.
  • Dropping brackets when substituting. For f(x) = x^2, f(-3) = 9, not -9.
  • Forgetting domain restrictions, especially the zero denominator, which often eliminates a choice.
  • 2f(x) against f(2x). One doubles the output, the other doubles the input.

Speed

  • Work from the inside out on composites.
  • Cover the x with your finger. Whatever is in the brackets goes wherever your finger was.
  • For "which function has this graph", test one easy point such as x = 0 and eliminate.
  • Inverse check: f(f^(-1)(x)) must give back x. A ten-second verification.

Summation results worth knowing

Recursive sequences. Given a_1 = 3 and a_(n+1) = 2 a_n - 1, just generate: 3, 5, 9, 17, 33. GRE recursive questions rarely run past six or eight terms, so computing beats finding a closed form.

Traps

  • Off by one in the nth term. It is a + (n-1)d. The first term uses no d at all.
  • Off by one in counting terms. From 24 to 198 in steps of 6 there are 30 terms, not 29.
  • Confusing arithmetic with geometric. Check differences and ratios before choosing a formula.
  • Using the infinite-sum formula when |r| >= 1. There is no finite sum.
  • Assuming a pattern from three terms. 1, 2, 4 could continue 8 or 7. Look for a stated rule.
  • Sum against term. "The 10th term" and "the sum of the first 10 terms" are different questions.

Speed

  • Sum = count * average, with average = (first + last)/2. One line that replaces both sum formulas for any evenly spaced set.
  • Count terms with (last - first)/step + 1. Say the "plus one" out loud every time.
  • For recursive sequences, list the terms. Almost always faster than algebra.
  • Look for telescoping. Sums like 1/(1*2) + 1/(2*3) + ... collapse, because each term is 1/n - 1/(n+1).

The equating rule has conditions. If x = 1 then 1^5 = 1^9 without 5 equaling 9.

Radical rules

sqrt(xy)  = sqrt(x) * sqrt(y)        for x, y >= 0
sqrt(x/y) = sqrt(x)/sqrt(y)          for x >= 0 and y > 0

Rationalizing. 1/sqrt(x) = sqrt(x)/x. For 1/(a + sqrt(b)), multiply above and below by the conjugate a - sqrt(b) to get (a - sqrt(b))/(a^2 - b).

Factoring with exponents — the highest-yield skill in the topic. 2^(n+2) + 2^n = 2^n(2^2 + 1) = 5 * 2^n.

Traps

  • Equating exponents when the base could be 0, 1 or -1.
  • x^a + x^b = x^(a+b). No such rule. Factor instead.
  • sqrt(x^2) = x. It is |x|.
  • Negative exponents read as negative numbers. x^(-2) = 1/x^2, positive for any non-zero real x.
  • Flipping a fractional exponent. x^(2/3) is the cube root of x^2, not x^(3/2).
  • Canceling a variable base that could be zero.

Speed

  • Rewrite every base as a prime power. Nearly all exponent equations become linear in the exponent.
  • Factor out the smallest power on any sum or difference of powers with the same base.
  • When the answer must be independent of n, factoring is the intended route.
  • Conjugates clear any two-term radical denominator in one step.
  • Pull out perfect squares first. sqrt(48) = 4 sqrt(3) before anything else.
  • Check with a number. Substitute x = 4 into your simplified form and the original; they must agree.

  • Averaging the two speeds. Valid only when the times are equal, never when the distances are.

  • Forgetting the head start in a catch-up problem.

  • Stops and breaks. A 20-minute halt still counts in the average speed for the whole trip.

  • Pick a convenient distance when none is given, usually the least common multiple of the speeds. For 60 and 90, take 180 kilometers each way: the times are 3 hours and 2 hours, so 360 kilometers in 5 hours, giving 72 kilometers per hour. No formula needed.
  • Over equal distances, the average speed always lands nearer the slower speed. Predict that before computing. (Over equal times it is exactly the midpoint.)
  • For meeting problems, add the speeds and divide the gap. One line.

4.3 Work and rate

Formulas and rules

Concept Formula
Individual rate 1 job / time taken
Working together R_A + R_B
Fill against drain R_fill - R_drain
Two workers taking a and b hours together ab/(a + b) hours
Work done in time t Rate * t
Remaining work 1 - (work already done)
Worker-days workers * days is conserved, at equal efficiency

Two methods, and which to use.

Fractional rates. A takes 6 hours, so the rate is 1/6 of the job per hour. B takes 3 hours, so 1/3. Together 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2, giving 2 hours.

Least-common-multiple units, usually faster. Let the job be LCM(6, 3) = 6 units. A does 1 unit an hour, B does 2, together 3, so 6/3 = 2 hours. No fractions at all.

Worker-days. If 6 workers take 10 days, the job is 60 worker-days, so 15 workers take 60/15 = 4 days.

Traps

  • Averaging the times. A at 6 hours and B at 3 hours do not take 4.5 hours together. The answer must be less than the faster worker's time.
  • Adding times. Never.
  • Forgetting the drain is negative. Subtract it.
  • Using worker-days when efficiencies differ. It assumes identical workers.
  • Skipping the sanity check. The combined time is always shorter than any individual time.
  • "Half the job" questions. Find the rate first, then apply it to half a job.

Speed

  • Use least-common-multiple units. It removes every fraction, and it is worth learning before anything else in this topic.
  • Two-worker shortcut ab/(a+b). For 12 and 18: 216/30 = 7.2 hours. Instant.
  • Check the bound. The combined time always lies between half the faster time and the faster time.
  • Worker-days is conserved, so more workers means proportionally fewer days.

4.5 Simple and compound interest

Throughout, r is the rate as a decimal (8 percent is 0.08) and t is in years, matching the rate's period.

Formulas and rules

Concept Formula
Simple interest SI = P * r * t
Amount, simple A = P(1 + rt)
Amount, compound A = P(1 + r)^t
Compound interest CI = P[(1 + r)^t - 1]
Compounded n times a year A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)
The 2-year difference CI - SI = P * r^2
Doubling time, roughly about 72 / (rate as a percent) years

Compounding frequency. For 12 percent a year compounded quarterly over 2 years: the rate per quarter is 3 percent and there are 8 periods, so A = P(1.03)^8.

The 2-year difference is worth memorizing. For P = 10,000 at 10 percent, the difference is 10,000 * 0.01 = 100.

Traps

  • Using the annual rate with a non-annual period. Quarterly compounding needs r/4 and 4t.
  • Forgetting to subtract the principal. "Interest earned" is A - P, not A.
  • Treating the percentage as a whole number. 8 percent is 0.08 in the formula.
  • Assuming they always differ. Simple and compound interest are equal for exactly one compounding period and diverge after that — a standard quantitative comparison setup.
  • Missing the word "compounded". If it is absent, the interest is usually simple.
  • "6 percent a year compounded semi-annually" is 3 percent per half-year.

Speed

  • Compute the multiplier, then raise it to the power. Keep (1.08)^3 unevaluated until the end.
  • For 2-year problems, use P r^2. Five seconds.
  • Year by year beats the formula for two or three years: add 10 percent, then 10 percent of the new total.
  • On comparison questions, compound beats simple for t > 1 — you may not need to compute at all.

  • Use multipliers for the whole chain in one line.
  • For reverse problems, divide by the multiplier — never subtract the same percent.
  • Equal profit and loss at x percent gives a net loss of (x/10)^2 percent. At 10 percent each way, a 1 percent loss.

Multiple stages — chain the multipliers against the right base. In a class of 200 where 60 percent are girls and 25 percent of the girls play a sport: girls are 120, and sporting girls are 30, which is 15 percent of the class, not 25.

  • Set the total to 100. Every answer then reads directly as a percent.
  • Convert everything to multipliers of the original before combining. "70 percent left, then 80 percent of that" is 0.56 of the original.
  • Draw a two-by-two table whenever two categories overlap.
  • To reverse a percent change, divide by the multiplier.
  • Check that the pieces sum to 100 percent at the end.

Section 5 — Geometry

The rule that governs the whole section. GRE geometric figures are not necessarily drawn to scale, so appearance is never a safe guide to a length or an angle. You may assume only that lines shown as straight are straight, that points on a line fall in the order shown, and that objects sit in the relative positions shown. A right angle, a pair of parallel lines, an equal pair of sides or a tangent must be marked or stated before you may use it.

The exceptions are worth knowing: coordinate systems, number lines and the graphs in data interpretation are drawn to scale. Geometric figures are not.

The shortcut that solves most of these figures. Mark one angle x. Every other angle in the figure is either x or 180 - x. Decide which from the transversal relationships — corresponding and alternate pairs are equal, same-side interior pairs are supplementary — never from how the angle looks.

Traps

  • Assuming lines are parallel because they look parallel. They must be marked or stated.
  • Alternate against same-side interior. Z means equal; C means supplementary.
  • Calling a 90 pair supplementary. Complementary is 90; supplementary is 180.
  • Measuring off the figure. A length or an angle that merely looks right is not a given.

Speed

  • Label one angle x and propagate. The whole figure falls out in seconds.
  • Extend lines in your sketch to reveal a transversal that was not drawn.
  • Draw an auxiliary parallel line through a bent vertex between two parallels; it turns a zig-zag into two simple transversal setups.
  • Sum-check at the end: straight lines to 180, points to 360.

The symmetry line. In an isosceles triangle, the altitude from the apex to the base is simultaneously the altitude, the median, the perpendicular bisector of the base and the apex angle bisector. Dropping it is the standard solution move — it splits the triangle into two congruent right triangles with a known half-base.

Traps

  • Assuming which angle is the apex. If the given angle is acute, two configurations may be possible, and the answer may be that the relationship cannot be determined.
  • Assuming isosceles from the picture. Look for tick marks.
  • Using s^2 sqrt(3)/4 on a merely isosceles triangle. It is for equilateral only.
  • Taking the base angles to equal the apex. They equal each other.
  • Equiangular against equilateral. Equivalent for triangles, but not for other polygons: a rectangle is equiangular and not equilateral.

Speed

  • Drop the altitude immediately.
  • (180 - apex)/2 for the base angles. One step.
  • If two angles are equal, mark the opposite sides equal at once. It often unlocks the length you need.
  • On "cannot be determined" questions, actively hunt for the second configuration.

5.6 Similar and congruent figures

Similarity tests, any one is enough: AA (two pairs of equal angles), SSS (all three side pairs in the same ratio), SAS (two side pairs in ratio with the included angles equal). AA is the workhorse, because two equal angle pairs force the third.

Congruence tests: SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS and RHS. SSA is not a valid test, and neither is AAA — AAA gives similarity, not congruence.

The scaling rule — the single most important line in this topic

lengths and perimeters scale as k
areas scale as k^2
volumes scale as k^3

Where similar triangles hide. A line parallel to one side of a triangle cuts off a similar triangle. The altitude to the hypotenuse of a right triangle creates two triangles similar to the original and to each other. Two lines crossing between a pair of parallels make an hourglass.

Setting up the proportion. Write the corresponding vertices in the same order: if triangle ABC is similar to triangle DEF, then AB/DE = BC/EF = AC/DF.

Traps

  • Using the linear ratio for areas. Sides 2:3 give areas 4:9. The number-one trap in the topic.
  • Using the area ratio for sides. Areas 16:25 give sides 4:5.
  • Mismatched correspondence. Pair the right vertices or the ratio is nonsense.
  • Assuming similarity from appearance.
  • Treating SSA as a congruence test.
  • Perimeter ratio. Perimeters scale like lengths, not like areas.

Speed

  • Look for parallel lines — they generate similar triangles automatically.
  • Look for a shared angle plus one more equal angle. That is AA in two seconds.
  • Redraw the two triangles separately, in the same orientation. Overlapping figures cause most correspondence errors.
  • Shadow, ladder and mirror problems are nearly always similarity.

The hierarchy. Every square is a rectangle and a rhombus; every rectangle and rhombus is a parallelogram. The reverse is never guaranteed.

The trapezoid midsegment joins the midpoints of the non-parallel sides and equals (a + b)/2. An isosceles trapezoid has equal non-parallel sides, equal base angles and equal diagonals.

The optimization fact. Among all rectangles with a fixed perimeter, the square has the largest area; among all with a fixed area, the square has the smallest perimeter. A frequent quantitative comparison theme.

Traps

  • Assuming a quadrilateral is a rectangle because it looks like one.
  • Doubling the side to get the diagonal. A square's diagonal is about 1.41s, not 2s.
  • Assuming equal perimeters mean equal areas, or the reverse. A 1-by-36 and a 6-by-6 rectangle both have area 36, with perimeters 74 and 24.
  • Taking a parallelogram's diagonals to be equal. They bisect each other; they are equal only in a rectangle.
  • Taking diagonals to be perpendicular. True in a rhombus and a kite, not in a general parallelogram.
  • Using a non-parallel side in the trapezoid formula, or dropping the 1/2.
  • Adding the diagonals instead of multiplying in the rhombus area.
  • Units. Carpeting needs area; fencing needs perimeter.

Speed

  • Square area from the diagonal: d^2/2. Skips the side entirely.
  • Rhombus area from the diagonals: half their product. Faster than base times height.
  • Say the trapezoid formula as "the average of the parallel sides, times the height". It sticks.
  • A rectangle's diagonal is often a Pythagorean triple.
  • The diagonals of a rhombus make four identical right triangles with legs equal to the half-diagonals, so Pythagoras gives the side instantly.
  • For maximum area or minimum perimeter, go square.

  • Pythagorean triples appear constantly in chord and tangent problems.

Reading the sign. Positive rises left to right; negative falls; zero is horizontal; undefined is vertical.

  • Inverting the formula. The change in y goes on top.

  • Giving a vertical line slope 0. Horizontal is 0; vertical is undefined.

  • Trusting visual steepness when the two axes have different scales.

  • Rewrite everything as y = mx + b. Slope and intercept then read straight off.
  • slope = -A/B for Ax + By = C. Instant, with no rearranging.
  • For intercepts, cover the other variable: set y = 0 for the x-intercept, x = 0 for the y-intercept.
  • Equal slopes plus a shared point means collinear; equal slopes with no shared point means parallel.

  • Forgetting to substitute back for the vertex's y-coordinate.
  • Assuming every parabola crosses the x-axis.
  • Confusing the y-intercept with the vertex. They coincide only when b = 0.
  • Reading "maximum" for an upward parabola.

Speed

  • Check the sign of a first. It settles maximum against minimum and often eliminates half the choices.
  • x = -b/(2a) is both the axis of symmetry and the vertex x. One formula, two uses.
  • The y-intercept is just c. No work at all.
  • For "how many real solutions", compute the discriminant only.
  • Match graphs to equations by testing x = 0 and checking which way it opens.

Section 7 — Statistics and Probability

7.1 Mean, median, mode and range

Measure Definition
Mean sum / count
Sum mean * count — the most useful rearrangement on the test
Median, odd count the middle value after sorting
Median, even count the average of the two middle values
Mode the most frequent value
Range largest - smallest

Sort before you take the median. Always. This is the most common careless error in the topic.

What transformations do

Transformation Mean, median, mode Range and standard deviation
Add k to every value each increases by k unchanged
Multiply every value by k each multiplied by k multiplied by \|k\|

Mean against median, and skew

  • Symmetric data: mean equals median.
  • Skewed right, a few very large values: mean above median.
  • Skewed left, a few very small values: mean below median.

Evenly spaced sets: mean = median = (first + last)/2. This shortcut appears constantly.

Traps

  • Finding the median without sorting.
  • Assuming the median is a member of the set. With an even count it usually is not.
  • Assuming a unique mode. A set can be bimodal, or have no mode at all.
  • Assuming adding a constant changes the range. It does not.
  • Averaging the averages. That works only when the groups are the same size; otherwise you need a weighted average.

Speed

  • Sum = mean * count. Almost every average word problem is solved by turning averages into sums.
  • To find a missing value, work with sums, not averages.
  • Deviation shortcut: to average numbers near 200, average their differences from 200 and add 200 back.
  • On "which measure changes" questions, test a tiny concrete set of three or five numbers rather than reasoning abstractly.
  • The median only cares about position, so changing an extreme value usually leaves it alone.

7.2 Weighted average

Concept Formula
Two groups (n1 a1 + n2 a2)/(n1 + n2)
Weights as fractions or percents w1 a1 + w2 a2, with w1 + w2 = 1
Group sum count * average
Alligation, for the ratio of counts n1 : n2 = (a2 - m) : (m - a1)

Alligation. The ratio of the counts is the inverse of the distances from the combined mean. Averages of 60 and 90 combining to 70: the distances are 90 - 70 = 20 and 70 - 60 = 10, so the counts are in the ratio 20 : 10 = 2 : 1. Twice as many in the 60 group, which is exactly why the mean sits nearer 60.

The sanity check. A weighted average always lies between the two group averages, and closer to the group with more members. It can equal them only when the two are equal to begin with. If your answer sits outside that range, you have made an error.

Traps

  • Averaging the averages. It is the planted wrong answer.
  • Weighting by the wrong quantity. Weight by the number of items, not by the values.
  • Inverting the alligation ratio. The larger group is the one whose average is closer to the combined mean.
  • Using percentages as weights without normalizing. They must sum to 1.
  • Omitting a group when there are three or more.

Speed

  • Convert averages to totals immediately. These are sum problems in disguise.
  • Use alligation when you need a ratio rather than a value. A ten-second method.
  • Check the pull direction first. If the larger group has the higher average, the answer must exceed the plain average — and that alone often identifies the choice.
  • Set the total count to 100 when the problem gives percentages.

7.3 Range and standard deviation

Concept Rule
Range max - min
Standard deviation roughly, the average distance from the mean
It is never negative and it is 0 only when every value is identical
Add k to every value range and standard deviation unchanged
Multiply every value by k both multiplied by \|k\|
Values spread wide larger standard deviation
Values clustered near the mean smaller standard deviation

Compare, do not compute. Ask which set has values further from its own mean.

{10, 10, 10, 10}  ->  standard deviation 0
{9, 10, 10, 11}   ->  small
{2, 6, 14, 18}    ->  large

The formula, for understanding rather than for use: sqrt[ (sum of (each value - mean)^2) / n ]. The GRE will not ask you to evaluate it under time pressure, but it explains why adding a constant changes nothing — every deviation is unchanged — and why multiplying by k scales it.

Traps

  • Assuming adding a constant increases the spread. It shifts everything equally.
  • Assuming a bigger mean means a bigger standard deviation. They are independent.
  • Assuming a bigger range means a bigger standard deviation. Usually true but not guaranteed: the range depends on two values, the standard deviation on all of them.
  • Computing when a comparison would do.
  • Expecting a negative value. Impossible.
  • Multiplying by a negative. It scales by |k|, so it stays positive.

Speed

  • "Adding shifts, multiplying scales." Two words cover every transformation question.
  • Standard deviation 0 means all values are equal. A frequent quantitative comparison shortcut.
  • The set with more values bunched near the middle has the smaller spread, even when the extremes match.
  • Range is a two-number question. Find the maximum and minimum and stop.

7.4 Quartiles, interquartile range and percentiles

Term Meaning
Q1 25th percentile, the median of the lower half
Q2 50th percentile, the median
Q3 75th percentile, the median of the upper half
Interquartile range Q3 - Q1
Range max - min

Each quartile section holds 25 percent of the data points. Between Q1 and Q3 lies the middle 50 percent.

Computing them. Sort. Find the median. Then Q1 is the median of the values below it and Q3 the median of the values above it. For 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12, 15: the median is 7.5, Q1 is 4.5, Q3 is 11, and the interquartile range is 6.5.

A boxplot runs from Q1 to Q3 with a line at the median, and the whiskers reach the minimum and maximum.

Percentile is a rank, not a score. Scoring in the 80th percentile means scoring higher than 80 percent of test takers — not getting 80 percent of the questions right. Worth knowing for your own score report as much as for the test.

The outlier rule, occasionally tested: a value lying more than 1.5 * the interquartile range below Q1 or above Q3.

Traps

  • Percentile read as a percentage score.
  • Assuming quartiles are evenly spaced numerically. Each holds 25 percent of the data points, not 25 percent of the numeric range.
  • Forgetting to sort.
  • Including the median in both halves. With an odd count, exclude it when finding Q1 and Q3.
  • Computing the interquartile range as Q3 + Q1.
  • Assuming the 50th percentile is the mean. It is the median.

Speed

  • Sort first, then count positions. These are counting exercises, not arithmetic.
  • Q1 and Q3 are just medians of the halves — the same procedure twice.
  • Percentile to count: multiply the percentile by the total.
  • A long whisker with a short box means skew.

7.5 Distributions and the normal curve

Shape Relationship The picture
Symmetric mean = median balanced bell
Skewed right mean above median long tail to the right
Skewed left mean below median long tail to the left

The memory hook: the tail pulls the mean. It settles every skew question in three seconds.

The normal distribution is perfectly symmetric and bell-shaped, with mean, median and mode all at the center, and it is described completely by its mean and standard deviation.

The 68-95-99.7 rule

Within Percent of the data
1 standard deviation of the mean about 68
2 standard deviations about 95
3 standard deviations about 99.7

Because the curve is symmetric, half of each band sits on each side. So about 34 percent lies between the mean and 1 standard deviation above it, about 13.5 percent between 1 and 2, and about 2.35 percent between 2 and 3. Those three numbers cover almost every GRE normal question.

Frequency tables. When data arrives as values with frequencies, the mean is a weighted average: multiply each value by its frequency, sum, and divide by the total frequency.

Traps

  • Reversing the skew direction. Skewed right means the tail points right and the mean exceeds the median, even though most of the data sits on the left.
  • Applying 68-95-99.7 to data that is not normal. The stem must say normally distributed.
  • Forgetting to halve. "5 percent outside 2 standard deviations" means 2.5 percent in each tail.
  • Reading a frequency as a value. Multiply; do not add frequencies to values.
  • Confusing the curve's height with probability. It is the area that gives the proportion.

Speed

  • Sketch the bell curve and mark the mean and the standard deviation boundaries. Every normal question then reads straight off the sketch.
  • Symmetry halves the work: the area below the mean is always 50 percent.
  • For frequency tables, keep a running total of value times frequency.

7.6 Counting and permutations

Concept Formula
Fundamental counting principle multiply the choices at each stage
Factorial n! = n(n-1)...(1), and 0! = 1
Arrangements of n distinct items n!
Permutations of r from n P(n,r) = n!/(n-r)!
Arrangements with repeats n! / (a! b! c! ...)
Circular arrangements of n (n-1)!

Factorials to know: 1, 2, 6, 24, 120, 720, 5040 for 1! through 7!.

Repeated letters. BANANA has 6 letters with A three times and N twice, so 6!/(3! * 2!) = 60 distinct arrangements.

The two restriction techniques. Glue when items must be together: treat the block as one item, arrange, then multiply by the internal arrangements of the block. Complement when items must be apart: total arrangements minus those where they are together.

Circular arrangements lose one degree of freedom, because rotations are the same arrangement.

Traps

  • Using a permutation where order does not matter. "How many committees of 3" is a combination.
  • Forgetting to divide by the repeats.
  • Adding instead of multiplying. "And" multiplies; only mutually exclusive alternatives, "or", add.
  • Treating 0! as 0. It is 1.
  • Counting circular arrangements as n!.
  • Double counting in restriction problems — glue or complement, not both at once.
  • Assuming repetition is or is not allowed. A three-digit code from 0 to 9 allows repeats, giving 1000, unless the stem says the digits are distinct, which gives 10 * 9 * 8 = 720.

Speed

  • Ask "does order matter?" before writing anything.
  • Draw slots. One blank per position, fill in the number of choices, multiply. This handles most GRE counting questions with no formula at all.
  • "At least one" almost always means the complement: total minus none.
  • Cancel factorials rather than expanding. 10!/8! = 90.
  • Check the size. Counting answers grow fast; a suspiciously small one usually means you added where you should have multiplied.

7.7 Combinations

Concept Formula
Combinations of r from n C(n,r) = n!/[r!(n-r)!]

The symmetry rule saves real time. C(10,8) = C(10,2) = 45. Always convert to the smaller r.

Value

C(8,2) 28

C(n,2) = n(n-1)/2 covers handshakes, pairings, line segments between points and polygon diagonals. It is the single most reusable counting formula on the test.

Separate pools multiply. Choosing 2 from 5 men and 3 from 6 women gives C(5,2) * C(6,3) = 10 * 20 = 200.

Traps

  • Using permutations for selections. A committee of 3 from 9 is 84, not P(9,3) = 504.
  • Forgetting the symmetry shortcut and grinding C(12,10) the long way.
  • Adding when you should multiply across separate pools.
  • Summing "at least" cases and missing one. Use the complement.
  • Treating identical items as distinct, or the reverse.
  • Choosing three in order for a ranked list. That is a permutation — re-read whether the roles differ.

Speed

  • Order matters, use P. Order does not, use C. Decide before writing.
  • Use symmetry to make r as small as possible.
  • Cancel before multiplying. Never compute 9! outright.
  • "At least one" means total minus none.
  • Sanity check: C(n,r) is always a whole number and never exceeds P(n,r).

7.8 Basic probability

Concept Rule
Range 0 <= P <= 1
Certain P = 1
Impossible P = 0
Complement P(not A) = 1 - P(A)
All outcomes sum to 1
Odds in favor of a : b P = a/(a + b)

The complement rule is the workhorse. Anything containing "at least one" should trigger it: P(at least one) = 1 - P(none).

The standard totals

Experiment Total outcomes
One coin 2
Two coins 4
n coins 2^n
One die 6
Two dice 36
A standard deck 52 cards, 4 suits of 13, 26 red, 26 black, 12 face cards

Two-dice sums. The most likely sum is 7, with 6 ways out of 36. The sums 2 and 12 have one way each. For a sum s from 2 to 7 the count is s - 1, and it mirrors downward after 7.

Traps

  • The wrong denominator. Two dice give 36 outcomes, not 11 or 12.
  • Forgetting that (3,6) and (6,3) are different outcomes.
  • A probability above 1. A sure sign of a counting error.
  • Adding probabilities of events that can both happen. Subtract the overlap.
  • Assuming replacement. "Two marbles are drawn" conventionally means without replacement.
  • Confusing odds with probability. Odds of 3 : 2 in favor means a probability of 3/5.

Speed

  • Count the total first. Get the denominator right and the rest usually follows.
  • "At least one" means 1 minus none. Automatic.
  • For sequential draws, multiply stage by stage, adjusting the counts each time.
  • Simplify as you go. Answer choices are in lowest terms.
  • Sanity-check the size: a likely event should come out well above 1/2.

7.9 Compound probability, independence and dependence

Situation Rule
A and B, independent P(A) * P(B)
A and B, dependent P(A) * P(B given A)
A or B, mutually exclusive P(A) + P(B)
A or B, in general P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B)
Not A 1 - P(A)
At least one 1 - P(none)
Neither A nor B 1 - P(A or B)
Test for independence P(B given A) = P(B)
With replacement independent
Without replacement dependent

Independent and mutually exclusive are different ideas, and students conflate them constantly. Independence is about multiplication: one event does not change the other's probability. Mutual exclusivity is about addition: the two cannot both happen. In fact two events with non-zero probability cannot be both — mutually exclusive events are strongly dependent, because if one happens the other definitely does not.

Conditional probability. P(B given A) is the probability of B once A has happened, so P(A and B) = P(A) * P(B given A), and rearranged, P(B given A) = P(A and B) / P(A).

With or without replacement. With replacement the pool resets and the draws are independent. Without replacement, both the favorable count and the total shrink.

The gambler's fallacy. Independent trials have no memory. After five heads, the probability of a head on the sixth toss is still 1/2. The GRE tests this directly.

Traps

  • Adding when you should multiply. "And" multiplies.
  • Forgetting to subtract the overlap in an "or" question. Double counting is the classic error, and a total above 1 is the tell.
  • Treating dependent draws as independent.
  • Assuming mutual exclusivity before checking for overlap.
  • Order in "one red and one blue" problems. If no order is specified, both count: (4/10)(6/9) + (6/10)(4/9).
  • Confusing P(A and B) with P(B given A). The conditional already assumes A happened, so its denominator is smaller.

Speed

  • Underline the connective word: and, or, at least, neither.
  • Ask: does the pool change? One question settles independent against dependent.
  • Draw the tree for two-stage problems. Each path multiplies; add the paths that qualify.
  • Write the fractions before simplifying so the shrinking denominators stay visible.
  • For conditional probability, restrict the universe. "Given that the student studies Spanish" makes the Spanish students your new total.
  • Sanity check: without replacement, the probability of repeating the same color is always lower than with replacement.

7.10 Overlapping sets

Two groups

In A or B        = A + B - (in both)
Total group      = (A only) + (B only) + (both) + (neither)
A only           = A - (both)
Neither          = Total - (A or B)
At least one     = Total - neither

Three groups

In at least one = A + B + C - (A and B) - (B and C) - (A and C) + (A and B and C)

The final term is added back because members of all three were subtracted one time too many.

The two-by-two table, for two yes-or-no attributes

B yes B no Total
A yes both A only A total
A no B only neither not-A total
Total B total not-B total grand total

Every row and column must add up, so filling in four of the nine numbers determines the rest — usually the grand total, the two category totals and the overlap. That self-checking property is why the table beats a Venn diagram for two-group problems.

Traps

  • Forgetting to subtract the overlap. 25 plus 18 giving 43 in a class of 40 is impossible, and that impossibility should be your signal.
  • "A only" read as "A". The 25 cricket players include the 8 who also play football, so cricket-only is 17.
  • Forgetting the "neither" category. It belongs to the total but not to "A or B".
  • Sign error in the three-group formula. The pairs subtract; the triple adds back.
  • Assuming the categories are exhaustive. They rarely are.

Speed

  • Use the two-by-two table for two groups. It builds an arithmetic check into every row and column.
  • Fill in the overlap first, then work outward by subtraction.
  • Set the total to 100 for percentage problems.
  • For three groups, fill the Venn diagram from the center outward: all three, then the pairs minus the center, then the singles.
  • Sanity check: no region can be negative, and the parts must sum to the total.

Section 8 — Data Interpretation

Every Quant section contains a data set. These questions are not hard mathematically; they are won and lost on reading the display correctly and on not computing more than you need.

8.1 The 40-second orientation

Before reading a single question, check five things. Forty seconds spent here saves two minutes across the set.

  1. Title — what is being measured?
  2. Axes and column headers — what are the variables?
  3. Units — thousands, millions, percent? The single most common trap.
  4. Scale — does the axis start at zero? A truncated axis exaggerates differences.
  5. Footnotes — the small print often redefines something, and it usually matters for at least one question.

The recurring calculations

Question type Method
Percent of total part/total * 100
Percent change (new - old)/old * 100 — always divide by the earlier value
Ratio of two values divide
Average across categories sum / count
"How many times greater" divide
"Approximately" estimate, after checking the answer spread

Traps

  • Ignoring the units. A y-axis reading "revenue in thousands" means a bar at 45 is 45,000.
  • Trusting a truncated axis. A chart starting at 90 makes a 5 percent difference look enormous. Read the values, not the picture.
  • Percent of the wrong total. "What percent of Region A's sales" is not "what percent of total sales".
  • Confusing percent change with percentage-point change.
  • Reading the adjacent bar, row or year. The classic time-pressure error.
  • Extrapolating. Unless the question says to project, do not assume a trend continues.
  • Over-precision. If the stem says "approximately", estimate.

Speed

  • Read the display before the questions.
  • Check the answer spread before computing. Widely spaced choices mean estimation is intended.
  • Write the extracted numbers down rather than holding them in your head while calculating.
  • For "which is greatest", compare visually to shortlist, then verify only the top two.
  • Compute a shared total once and reuse it across all the questions in the set.
  • The calculator is there, but reaching for it costs time. 180/600 is a mental step: rewrite awkward numbers as fractions, so 180/600 = 3/10 = 30 percent.

The topic's most reliable trap. In a stacked bar, a middle segment's value is the difference between its top and bottom edges, not the height of its top edge.

  • Reading a stacked segment's top edge as its value.
  • A non-zero axis origin.
  • Confusing greatest value with greatest increase.
  • Misreading which series is which in a grouped chart. Check the legend before every question.
  • Assuming equal intervals on the horizontal axis. Occasionally the years skip.
  • Interpolating between points as if the data were continuous. The line joins measured points; intermediate values are not data.

Speed

  • Mark the legend before you start. A five-second habit that prevents series mix-ups.
  • For "greatest change", look for the steepest segment rather than computing every difference.
  • For stacked bars, write each segment's value in the margin once and reuse it across the set.
  • Estimate to the nearest gridline when the stem says "approximately".

Cross-referencing is where tables bite. Many questions need two cells from different rows and columns. Locate both and write them down before computing — that single habit prevents the wrong-row error.

Traps

  • Reading the adjacent row or column.
  • Mixed units across columns. One in thousands, another in units.
  • Using the grand total where a row total is needed, or the reverse.

8.5 Multi-display sets

The three-step method. Identify what the question wants — a value, a percent, a ratio or a change. Decide which display holds each ingredient and write them down separately. Then combine, usually by multiplying.

The key conversion

absolute value = share (from the pie) * total (from the bar or table)

Percent change across two displays. Build both years' absolute values first, then apply (new - old)/old. Do not try to combine the share's percent change with the total's in your head.

Multipliers compose, percent changes do not add. A 25 percent rise in the share alongside a 20 percent rise in the total gives 1.25 * 1.20 = 1.50, a 50 percent rise, not 45. If the share stays constant, the category's percent change simply equals the total's.

Traps

  • Applying one year's pie chart to a different year. Shares change unless stated otherwise.
  • Adding percent changes.
  • Using the wrong total when two bar charts are present.
  • Mixing units across displays.
  • Answering from one display when both are needed. That is the whole design of these sets — if a question feels unanswerable, you are probably missing the second display.
  • Assuming a relationship the data does not state, such as constant shares or linear growth.

Speed

  • Label each display in the margin once with what it supplies: "shares", "totals".
  • Convert shares to absolute values once, then answer the whole set from your converted numbers.
  • Write intermediate values down. Holding four numbers from two charts in your head is exactly where the errors happen.

Section 9 — The 25 traps that cost the most marks

Every one of these is an error students make after they have understood the topic. If you read nothing else the night before, read this page.

Reading the question

  1. Solving for x when the question asked for 2x, or for x + y. Underline what is asked before you start, and read the last line again before you answer.
  2. Answering a multiple-answer question with one selection. Square boxes mean select all. Check the button shape before you answer.
  3. Stopping after two correct options. There may be a third, and there is no partial credit.
  4. Rounding when no rounding was asked for, or not rounding when it was. Numeric entry has no safety net.

Assuming what was not stated

  1. Assuming a variable is a positive integer. Unless the stem says so, x can be negative, zero or a fraction. "Number" does not mean "integer".
  2. Assuming a figure is to scale in quantitative comparison, or assuming a right angle, parallel lines or equal sides that were never marked.
  3. Choosing "cannot be determined" when both quantities are pure numbers. Impossible: the relationship is fixed even if you cannot see it.
  4. Testing only one value on quantitative comparison. One test can rule things out; it can never prove a relationship. Run the FROZEN check.

Algebra

  1. (a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2. The 2ab is missing. The most common algebra error on the test.
  2. Losing the negative root. x^2 = 49 gives 7 and -7. And sqrt(x^2) = |x|, not x.
  3. Dividing by a variable that could be zero. From x^2 = 3x, dividing by x loses x = 0. Factor instead.
  4. Forgetting to flip the inequality when multiplying or dividing by a negative — and dividing by a variable whose sign you do not know.
  5. Sum of the roots written as b/a. It is -b/a.
  6. Canceling across a plus sign. (x + 3)/3 is not x. You may cancel factors, never terms.

Percent, ratio and rate

  1. Percent change computed on the new value. Always divide by the original.
  2. Adding successive percents. Multiply the multipliers: a 20 percent fall then a 20 percent rise leaves you 4 percent down, and recovering a 20 percent fall needs a 25 percent rise.
  3. "Percent of the remainder" read as "percent of the original".
  4. Part-to-part read as part-to-whole. 2 : 3 means 2/5 of the whole, not 2/3.
  5. Averaging the speeds. Average speed is total distance over total time: add the distances and add the times, never the speeds. In work problems it is the other way round — there the rates add and the times do not.
  6. Averaging two averages of unequal groups. You need a weighted average, and the result always sits closer to the bigger group.

Geometry, counting and data

  1. Using the linear ratio for areas. Sides 2 : 3 give areas 4 : 9 and volumes 8 : 27. Likewise, doubling the radius quadruples the area.
  2. Using the diameter as the radius, or using a slanted side as a height.
  3. Using a permutation where order does not matter — and forgetting that "at least one" means one minus the probability of none.
  4. Forgetting to subtract the overlap. P(A or B) subtracts P(A and B), and two groups overlap unless you are told otherwise. A probability above 1 or a group total above the class size is the tell.
  5. Ignoring the units on a data display, reading a stacked segment's top edge as its value, or comparing percentages across two pie charts with different totals.
