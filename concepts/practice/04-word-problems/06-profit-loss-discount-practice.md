# Profit, Loss & Discount — Practice Set

**Bank code:** `WRD-PNL` · **Section:** Word Problems · **30 questions** — Easy 5 · Medium 8 · Hard 12 · Extreme 5

**Lecture capsule:** [06-profit-loss-discount.md](../../04-word-problems/06-profit-loss-discount.md)

> Three prices and one question: profit percent is on cost price, discount percent is on marked price, and successive percentages multiply rather than add.

## Part C — Worked Solutions

### Q1 · `WRD-PNL-001` — Answer: D

Step 1 — identify the two prices. Cost price CP = $80, selling price SP = $100.
Step 2 — profit = SP - CP = 100 - 80 = $20.
Step 3 — profit percent is always measured on the COST price, not on the selling price: profit% = (profit/CP)*100.
Step 4 — profit% = (20/80)*100 = 0.25*100 = 25%.
Check: if 25% is right, then SP = CP*(1 + 25/100) = 80*1.25 = 100, which is exactly the selling price given. Correct.
Answer: D.

**Trap.** Dividing the $20 profit by the SELLING price, 20/100 = 20%, gives choice B. That is the signature error of this whole topic: profit percent has cost price as its base, never selling price. Dividing by the average of the two prices, 20/90 = 22.22%, gives choice C, and dividing by their sum, 20/180 = 11.11%, gives choice A. Choice E, 80%, is the cost price as a percent of the selling price (80/100), which answers a different question entirely.

### Q2 · `WRD-PNL-005` — Answer: D

Step 1 — the 25 percent loss is measured on the COST price, which is the unknown. So SP = CP*(1 - 25/100) = 0.75*CP.
Step 2 — substitute the selling price: 90 = 0.75*CP.
Step 3 — divide, never subtract: CP = 90/0.75 = $120.
Check: a $120 cost and a $90 sale lose $30, and 30/120 = 0.25, exactly 25 percent of cost. Correct.
Answer: D.

**Trap.** Adding 25 percent to the $90 selling price, 90*1.25 = $112.50 (choice C), which measures the loss against the selling price instead of the cost price. Choice A, $67.50, takes 25 percent off the $90 and moves in the wrong direction entirely. Choice B, $72, remembers to divide but divides by the wrong multiplier: 90/1.25 = 72, using 1 + 0.25 where a loss requires 1 - 0.25. Choice E, $135, is the reciprocal-multiplier slip: the student reads the 25 percent loss as 'sold at two-thirds of cost' and divides by 2/3, giving 90/(2/3) = 135, instead of dividing by the correct 3/4. The unknown is the base, so the selling price must be DIVIDED by 0.75.

### Q3 · `WRD-PNL-006` — Answer: 25

Step 1 — profit per orange = 3.00 - 2.40 = $0.60.
Step 2 — the base for profit percent is the cost price, $2.40.
Step 3 — profit% = (0.60/2.40)*100 = 0.25*100 = 25.
Check: 2.40*1.25 = 3.00, the selling price given. Correct.
Answer: 25.

**Trap.** Dividing the $0.60 profit by the $3.00 selling price gives 20, the classic wrong-base slip; profit percent is computed on cost price. Reading the question as 'the selling price is what percent of the cost price' gives 125.

### Q4 · `WRD-PNL-008` — Answer: C

Step 1 — the discount percent is measured on the MARKED price: discount = 0.20*500 = $100.
Step 2 — selling price SP = 500 - 100 = $400.
Step 3 — profit = SP - CP = 400 - 320 = $80.
Step 4 — the profit percent is measured on the COST price: profit% = (80/320)*100 = 25%.
Check: 320*1.25 = 400, which is the selling price found in Step 2. Correct.
Answer: C.

**Trap.** Two different bases appear in one question and each has its own wrong-base answer. Dividing the $80 profit by the $400 selling price gives 20%, choice B; dividing it by the $500 marked price gives 16%, choice A. Forgetting the discount entirely and treating $500 as the selling price gives a profit of $180, which is 56.25% of cost (choice E) or 36% of the marked price (choice D).

### Q5 · `WRD-PNL-013` — Answer: B, C, E

Step 1 — the actual sale: profit = 250 - 200 = $50, and the base is the cost price, so profit% = (50/200)*100 = 25.
A. FALSE. 20 is 50/250, the profit divided by the selling price. The base must be cost price.
B. TRUE. 50/200 = 0.25, so the profit percent is 25.
C. TRUE. Selling at $150 loses 200 - 150 = $50, and 50/200 = 0.25, a loss percent of 25.
D. FALSE. A cost of $180 and a sale at $215 give a profit of $35, and 35/180 = 0.1944, about 19.4 percent, not 25 percent. To match 25 percent the sale price would have to be 180*1.25 = $225.
E. TRUE. A 40 percent profit means SP = 200*(1 + 40/100) = 200*1.4 = $280.
Check: 280 - 200 = 80 and 80/200 = 0.40, confirming E; 225 would be needed for D, so $215 is short. Correct.
Answer: B, C and E.

**Trap.** Option A is the signature error of the topic: dividing the $50 profit by the $250 selling price to get 20 percent. Option D invites the assumption that any $35-ish profit on a slightly smaller cost 'must be about the same percent'; the arithmetic 35/180 = 19.4 percent settles it and shows $225, not $215, is the matching price.

### Q6 · `WRD-PNL-018` — Answer: C

Step 1 — Dealer P's marked price is 40 percent above his $200 cost: 200*1.40 = $280. The 30 percent discount is taken on that marked price, not on cost: 280*0.70 = $196.
Step 2 — check the net multiplier. 1.40*0.70 = 0.98, so P's selling price is 98 percent of cost, that is 200*0.98 = $196, a 2 percent LOSS. A 40 percent markup followed by a 30 percent discount does not leave a 10 percent profit, because the discount's base is the larger marked price.
Step 3 — Dealer Q sells at a 5 percent profit on cost: 200*1.05 = $210.
Step 4 — the gap is 210 - 196 = $14.
Check: P loses $4 and Q gains $10, so Q is ahead by 4 + 10 = $14. Correct.
Answer: C.

**Trap.** Reading the 40 percent markup and 30 percent discount as a net 10 percent gain, which would make P's price $220 and put P ahead by $10 (choice B). Percentages taken on different bases do not add; the multipliers 1.40 and 0.70 give 0.98, a 2 percent loss. Choice A, $6, comes from correctly finding the 2 percent but applying it in the wrong direction, treating $204 as P's price.

### Q7 · `WRD-PNL-020` — Answer: E

Step 1 — put both prices on the same unit, one apple.
Step 2 — cost price per apple = 6/10 = $0.60.
Step 3 — selling price per apple = 6/8 = $0.75.
Step 4 — profit per apple = 0.75 - 0.60 = $0.15, and the base is the cost price: profit% = (0.15/0.60)*100 = 25.
Check with a convenient quantity: 40 apples cost 4*6 = $24 and sell for 5*6 = $30, a profit of $6, and 6/24 = 0.25 = 25%. Correct.
Answer: E.

**Trap.** Dividing the $0.15 profit by the $0.75 selling price gives 20 percent, choice D, the wrong-base slip. Reading the two rates backwards, as if he bought 8 for $6 and sold 10 for $6, turns the deal into a loss: 0.15/0.75 = 20 percent gives choice B and 0.15/0.60 = 25 percent gives choice A. Choice C comes from noticing that $6 appears on both sides and concluding nothing changed, which ignores that the quantities differ.

### Q8 · `WRD-PNL-023` — Answer: D

Step 1 — convert K's rule to a cost-price rule so the two are comparable. For K, profit = 0.25*SP and profit = SP - CP, so CP = 0.75*SP and profit = 0.25*SP = (1/3)*CP. K's profit is 33.33 percent of K's cost, which is a higher RATE than J's 25 percent.
Step 2 — but the question asks for dollars, and the two cost prices are never linked. Let J cost $c and K cost $k. Then J's profit is 0.25c and K's profit is k/3.
Step 3 — take c = 300 and k = 300: J's profit is $75 and K's profit is $100, so Quantity B is greater.
Step 4 — now take c = 600 and k = 300: J's profit is $150 and K's profit is $100, so Quantity A is greater.
Step 5 — both orders occur, so the relationship cannot be determined.
Check the second case against K's own rule: CP = 300 and profit = 100 give SP = $400, and 100/400 = 0.25, exactly 25 percent of the selling price, as required. Correct.
Answer: D.

**Trap.** Answering B after the genuine insight that 25 percent of the SELLING price is a bigger bite than 25 percent of the cost price, 33.33 percent of cost against 25 percent. That comparison is between RATES; the columns ask for DOLLARS, and nothing in the stem says the two items cost the same. A larger rate on a smaller cost can lose to a smaller rate on a larger cost. Answering A instead comes from assuming J must be the more expensive item.

### Q9 · `WRD-PNL-026` — Answer: 56.25

Step 1 — set CP = 100. A 25 percent profit on cost means SP = 100*1.25 = 125.
Step 2 — the discount is taken on the marked price: SP = MP*(1 - 20/100) = 0.8*MP.
Step 3 — so 0.8*MP = 125, giving MP = 125/0.8 = 156.25.
Step 4 — the markup percent is measured on cost price: (156.25 - 100)/100 * 100 = 56.25.
Check: 100*1.5625 = 156.25, and 156.25*0.8 = 125, which is a 25 percent profit on a cost of 100. Correct.
Answer: 56.25.

**Trap.** Adding the percentages, 25 + 20 = 45, is the common wrong answer; it assumes the 20 percent discount is taken on the cost price rather than on the larger marked price, so it under-marks the goods. Marking up 45 percent gives MP = 145 and SP = 145*0.8 = 116, a profit of only 16 percent.

### Q10 · `WRD-PNL-027` — Answer: C

Step 1 — the profit is stated on the SELLING price: profit = 0.20*1200 = $240.
Step 2 — cost price = SP - profit = 1200 - 240 = $960.
Step 3 — the profit percent asked for uses COST price as the base: (240/960)*100 = 25.
Check: 960*1.25 = 1200, matching the selling price given. Correct.
Answer: C.

**Trap.** Choice B, 20%, is the answer of a student who assumes a percent on selling price and a percent on cost price are the same number; they never are when there is a profit. Choice A, 16.67%, reverses the conversion by computing 0.20/1.20 instead of 0.20/0.80 - that is the markup-to-margin direction, not the margin-to-markup direction asked for here. Choice D, 33.33%, comes from 240/720, subtracting the $240 profit from the cost a second time before dividing. Choice E, 125%, is the selling price as a percent of the cost price, 1200/960, reported without subtracting the cost first.
