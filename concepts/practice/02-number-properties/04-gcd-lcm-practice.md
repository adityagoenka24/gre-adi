**Lecture capsule:** [04-gcd-lcm.md](../../02-number-properties/04-gcd-lcm.md)

> GCD takes the lowest powers of the shared primes and answers grouping questions; LCM takes the highest powers of every prime and answers coinciding questions; and for two numbers GCD x LCM = the product.

Every question below is live in the question bank under the ID shown — the sheet and the portal are the same questions. Attempt a level with the clock running, then check Part C.

## Part C — Worked Solutions

### Q1 · `NUM-GCL-001` — Answer: C

Step 1 — prime factorize both. 12 = 2^2 x 3 and 18 = 2 x 3^2.
Step 2 — the greatest common divisor takes the LOWEST power of each SHARED prime. The shared primes are 2 and 3.
Lowest power of 2: 2^1. Lowest power of 3: 3^1.
Step 3 — multiply: greatest common divisor = 2 x 3 = 6.
Check: 12/6 = 2 and 18/6 = 3, both integers, and the greatest common divisor of 2 and 3 = 1, so nothing larger than 6 can divide both.
Answer: C, 6.

**Trap.** Choosing 36 (option E) computes the least common multiple, 2^2 x 3^2, by taking the highest power of each prime. A greatest common divisor can never exceed the smaller number, so 36 is impossible on sight. Choosing 3 (option B) stops at one shared prime and forgets the shared 2. Choosing 9 (option D) uses 3^2, but 12 contains only one 3.

### Q2 · `NUM-GCL-011` — Answer: 18

Step 1 — 'when do the two cycles next coincide' is an least common multiple question, not a greatest common divisor question.
Step 2 — prime factorize: 6 = 2 x 3 and 9 = 3^2.
Step 3 — the least common multiple takes the highest power of every prime present: 2^1 x 3^2 = 18.
Check: Bus A departs at 6, 12, 18; Bus B departs at 9, 18. The first shared minute is 18.
Answer: 18.

**Trap.** Answering 3, which is the greatest common divisor of 6 and 9. Greatest common divisor answers 'how large can the equal groups be'; least common multiple answers 'when do the cycles line up again'. Answering 54 multiplies 6 x 9: that is a common multiple, but not the least one, because 6 and 9 already share a factor of 3.

### Q3 · `NUM-GCL-012` — Answer: C

Step 1 — the least common multiple of a and b is by definition a multiple of b. If that least common multiple equals a, then a is a multiple of b.
Step 2 — 'a is a multiple of b' is the same statement as 'b divides a', so C is forced.
Step 3 — test the others on a = 12, b = 6, where the least common multiple of 12 and 6 = 12 = a. Here a is not equal to b, so A fails; a does not divide b, so D fails; the greatest common divisor of 12 and 6 = 6, not 1, so E fails.
Step 4 — B fails on a = b = 6, which also satisfies the least common multiple of a and b = a but has a not greater than b.
Check: when b divides a, the multiples of b already contain a, so the least common multiple is a itself.
Answer: C.

**Trap.** Picking A (a = b) or B (a > b): both describe cases that satisfy the condition, but neither is forced, since a = 12, b = 6 kills A and a = b = 6 kills B. Picking D reverses the divisibility - if a divided b the least common multiple would be b, not a.

### Q4 · `NUM-GCL-013` — Answer: A, B, C, D

Step 1 — prime factorize: 30 = 2 x 3 x 5 and 45 = 3^2 x 5.
Step 2 — greatest common divisor = lowest power of each shared prime = 3 x 5 = 15. Every common divisor of 30 and 45 is a divisor of 15.
Step 3 — the divisors of 15 are 1, 3, 5, 15. Now test each option.
A - 1 divides every integer. True.
B - 30/3 = 10 and 45/3 = 15. True.
C - 30/5 = 6 and 45/5 = 9. True.
D - 30/15 = 2 and 45/15 = 3. True.
E - 45/30 = 1.5, not an integer. False.
Check: the largest true option is 15, which matches the greatest common divisor of 30 and 45 = 15.
Answer: A, B, C, D.

**Trap.** Selecting only D because 15 is the greatest common divisor. The question asks for common divisors, and every divisor of the greatest common divisor is one. Selecting E treats 30 as common because it is one of the two numbers, but 30 does not divide 45.

### Q5 · `NUM-GCL-014` — Answer: C

Step 1 — distinct primes share no prime factor, so the greatest common divisor of p and q = 1.
Step 2 — the least common multiple of p and q equals (p x q) divided by their greatest common divisor, so it is (p x q) / 1 = p x q.
Step 3 — the columns are therefore equal for every choice of distinct primes.
Check: p = 3, q = 5 gives a least common multiple of 15 and 3 x 5 = 15. p = 2, q = 7 gives a least common multiple of 14 and 2 x 7 = 14.
Answer: C.

**Trap.** Answering D because p and q are unspecified. Coprimality alone fixes the relationship, whatever the primes are. Answering B comes from the reflex that a least common multiple is smaller than the product - true only when the two numbers share a factor, which distinct primes never do.

### Q6 · `NUM-GCL-019` — Answer: C

Step 1 — let d = the greatest common divisor of n and n + 1. Then d divides n and d divides n + 1.
Step 2 — anything dividing both also divides their difference: (n + 1) - n = 1.
Step 3 — the only positive integer that divides 1 is 1, so d = 1.
Step 4 — this argument uses nothing about n, so the columns are equal for every positive integer n.
Check: the greatest common divisor of 8 and 9 = 1, the greatest common divisor of 35 and 36 = 1, the greatest common divisor of 100 and 101 = 1.
Answer: C.

**Trap.** Answering A for large n, reasoning that big numbers must share big factors. Consecutive integers are always coprime; one of them being even does not help, because the other is then odd. Answering D because n is unspecified misses that the difference argument pins d to 1 for every n.

### Q7 · `NUM-GCL-021` — Answer: 60

Step 1 — prime factorize each: 12 = 2^2 x 3, 15 = 3 x 5, 20 = 2^2 x 5.
Step 2 — the least common multiple takes the highest power of every prime that appears anywhere.
Prime 2: highest exponent 2, so 2^2.
Prime 3: highest exponent 1, so 3.
Prime 5: highest exponent 1, so 5.
Step 3 — least common multiple = 4 x 3 x 5 = 60.
Check: 60/12 = 5, 60/15 = 4, 60/20 = 3, all integers. The next smaller candidate, 30, fails because 30/20 = 1.5.
Answer: 60.

**Trap.** Multiplying all three, 12 x 15 x 20 = 3600. That is a common multiple but nowhere near the least, because the three numbers share factors. Answering 1 computes the greatest common divisor of 12, 15 and 20 instead, which is indeed 1 - the greatest common divisor and the least common multiple of a set are opposite ends of the same table.

### Q8 · `NUM-GCL-024` — Answer: D

### Q9 · `NUM-GCL-026` — Answer: C

Step 1 — write a = 4m and b = 4n where m and n have greatest common divisor 1. Since a and b are at most 20, both m and n are at most 5.
Step 2 — the least common multiple of a and b = 4 x m x n, so maximizing the least common multiple means maximizing m x n over coprime pairs with m, n <= 5.
Step 3 — the largest such product is m = 4, n = 5, since the greatest common divisor of 4 and 5 = 1 and 4 x 5 = 20. The pair m = 5, n = 5 is not allowed because the greatest common divisor of 5 and 5 = 5.
Step 4 — least common multiple = 4 x 20 = 80, reached at a = 16 and b = 20.
Check: the greatest common divisor of 16 and 20 = 4 and the least common multiple of 16 and 20 = 80, and 4 x 80 = 320 = 16 x 20. Both numbers are at most 20. Correct.
Answer: C, 80.

**Trap.** Choosing 20 (option A) applies the greatest common divisor range rule to the least common multiple: the greatest common divisor lies between 1 and min(a, b), but the least common multiple runs the other way, max(a, b) <= least common multiple <= a x b, so it may be far larger than 20. Choosing 60 (option B) stops at the coprime pair m = 3, n = 5 and misses that m = 4, n = 5 is also coprime. Choosing 100 (option D) takes a = b = 20 and applies (greatest common divisor) x (least common multiple) = a x b as 400/4, but the greatest common divisor of 20 and 20 = 20, not 4. Choosing 320 (option E) uses least common multiple = a x b = 16 x 20; that equality holds only when a and b are coprime, and here they share a factor of 4.

### Q12 · `NUM-GCL-033` — Answer: A, B, D

A - the least common multiple is a multiple of a, and the smallest positive multiple of a is a itself, so the least common multiple of a and b >= a. True.
B - a x b is always a common multiple of a and b, and the least common multiple is the LEAST common multiple, so it cannot exceed a x b. True.
C - equality holds only when the greatest common divisor of a and b = 1. Take a = 4, b = 6: least common multiple = 12 but a x b = 24. False.
D - the greatest common divisor of a and b divides a, and a divides the least common multiple of a and b, so the greatest common divisor divides the least common multiple. True.
E - the inequality is strict, and that fails when a = b: the greatest common divisor of 6 and 6 = 6 and the least common multiple of 6 and 6 = 6, equal, not less. False.
Check on a = 4, b = 6: greatest common divisor = 2, least common multiple = 12. Indeed 12 >= 4, 12 <= 24, and 12 is a multiple of 2.
Answer: A, B, D.

**Trap.** Selecting C because least common multiple = a x b is the case students meet most often - it is exactly the coprime case, and it breaks the moment a and b share a factor, as 4 and 6 do. Selecting E is the 'greatest common divisor is always smaller than least common multiple' reflex; the two coincide precisely when a = b.

### Q13 · `NUM-GCL-035` — Answer: C

Any common divisor of m and n also divides m - n, so it is a common divisor of m - n and n. Conversely, any common divisor of m - n and n also divides (m - n) + n = m, so it is a common divisor of m and n. The two pairs have exactly the same common divisors, hence the same greatest one, so the two quantities are equal. Check: m = 48, n = 18 gives 6 for both (48 and 18; 30 and 18). With m = 35, n = 14, both are 7. Answer: C.

**Trap.** Answering A because m is larger than m - n, assuming a larger number gives a larger greatest common divisor; subtracting n removes nothing that m and n share. Answering D because m and n are not specified misses that the identity holds for every such pair.

12 * n = 4 * 60 = 240.
n = 20.

**Trap.** Entering 60/12 = 5 or 60/4 = 15. Neither has greatest common divisor 4 with 12. The two given values must be multiplied together and divided by 12.

### Q15 · `NUM-GCL-053` — Answer: B

Step 1 — greatest common divisor of all three takes the LOWEST exponent of each prime shared by all three.
Prime 2: min(3, 2, 1) = 1. Prime 3: min(2, 1, 3) = 1. Prime 5: min(1, 2, 1) = 1. Prime 7 is absent from a and c.
So greatest common divisor = 2 x 3 x 5 = 30.
Step 2 — least common multiple takes the HIGHEST exponent of every prime present.
Prime 2: max(3, 2, 1) = 3. Prime 3: max(2, 1, 3) = 3. Prime 5: max(1, 2, 1) = 2. Prime 7: 1.
So least common multiple = 2^3 x 3^3 x 5^2 x 7 = 8 x 27 x 25 x 7 = 37,800.
Step 3 — Quantity A = 30 x 37,800 = 1,134,000.
Step 4 — Quantity B: a = 360, b = 2,100, c = 270, so a x b x c = 360 x 2,100 x 270 = 204,120,000.
Step 5 — 204,120,000 is far larger, so Quantity B is greater.
Check by exponents alone, on the prime 2: Quantity A carries 1 + 3 = 4 copies of it, Quantity B carries 3 + 2 + 1 = 6. Quantity B wins on that prime, and the same happens on 3 and on 5.
Answer: B.

**Trap.** Answering C by applying greatest common divisor x least common multiple = product. That identity holds for TWO numbers only. With three numbers the greatest common divisor takes each prime's minimum exponent and the least common multiple takes its maximum, so the middle exponent is never counted at all. The one-second sanity check is 2, 4, 8: greatest common divisor = 2, least common multiple = 8, and 2 x 8 = 16, but 2 x 4 x 8 = 64.
