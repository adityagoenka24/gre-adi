2. Must-Know Rules

The 40-second orientation, before reading any question:

  1. Title — what is being measured?
  2. Axes / column headers — what are the variables?
  3. Units — thousands? millions? percent? The single most common trap.
  4. Scale — does the axis start at zero? A truncated axis exaggerates differences.
  5. Footnotes — small print often redefines something.

The recurring calculations:

Question type Formula
Percent of total part/total x 100
Percent change (new - old)/old x 100
Ratio of two values just divide
Average across categories sum / count
"How many times greater" divide (and note: "3 times greater" is ambiguous — the GRE means 3x)
Approximate value estimate; check the answer spread first

Two charts in one set. If a set gives you a pie chart of shares and a bar chart of totals, most questions require both: convert a share to an absolute value by multiplying by the relevant total.

3. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Percent of total

A pie chart shows a company's expenses: Salaries 40%, Rent 15%, Marketing 20%, Other 25%. Total expenses are $2.4 million. How much is spent on marketing?

0.20 x 2,400,000 = $480,000.

Example 2 — Percent change from a graph

A bar graph shows revenue of 250 units in 2023 and 325 units in 2024. What is the percent increase?

(325 - 250)/250 = 75/250 = 0.30 = 30%.

Note: divide by the old value.

Example 3 — Combining two displays

A pie chart shows region shares of sales (North 30%, South 45%, East 25%). A bar chart shows total sales by year: 2023 = $8m, 2024 = $10m. Assuming the shares held constant, by how much did North's sales grow?

2023 North: 0.30 x 8 = $2.4m. 2024 North: 0.30 x 10 = $3.0m.

Growth = $0.6m, a 25% increase (which equals the total's growth rate, since the share was constant).

4. GRE Traps

  • Ignoring the units. A y-axis labelled "revenue (in thousands)" means a bar at 45 represents 45,000.
  • Truncated axes. A bar chart starting at 90 makes a 5% difference look enormous. Read the values, not the picture.
  • Percent of the wrong total. "What percent of Region A's sales" is not "what percent of total sales".
  • Confusing percent change with percentage-point change.
  • Answering from the wrong year or category. Under time pressure, students read the adjacent bar.
  • Assuming a trend continues. Unless the question says to project, do not extrapolate.
  • Over-precision. If the question says "approximately", estimate; if the choices are far apart, do not compute exactly.

5. Speed Tricks

  • Read the chart before the questions. 40 seconds spent orienting saves two minutes across three questions.
  • Check the answer spread before computing. Widely spaced choices mean estimation is intended.
  • Write the extracted numbers down rather than holding them in your head while calculating.
  • Percent change: always divide by the earlier value.
  • For "which is greatest" questions, compare visually first, then verify only the top two candidates.
  • The on-screen calculator is available in the Quant section — but reaching for it costs time. Estimate first, calculate only when the answer choices demand precision.

6. Self-Check

Q1. A category is 18% of a total of 45,000. What is its value?

Q2. A value rises from 80 to 92. What is the percent increase?

Q3. Sales are 250 (Product A) and 400 (Product B). A is what percent of the combined total?

Answers

A1. 0.18 x 45,000 = 8,100.

A2. 12/80 = 0.15 = 15%.

A3. 250/650 = 0.3846 = about 38.5%.

7. One-Line Summary for the Board

Read the title, axes and units before the questions. Percent change always divides by the old value.
