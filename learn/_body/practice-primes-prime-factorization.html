Q1 · NUM-PRF-011 — Answer: B, C, E

A prime is a positive integer with exactly two positive factors: 1 and itself. A - 1 has only one positive factor, itself. It fails the 'exactly two' test, so 1 is neither prime nor composite. FALSE. B - 2 has factors 1 and 2 only. It is prime, and it is the only even prime. TRUE. C - 17: sqrt(17) is about 4.1, so only 2 and 3 need testing. 17 is odd and 1 + 7 = 8 is not a multiple of 3. TRUE. D - 51: the digit sum 5 + 1 = 6 is divisible by 3, so 51 = 3 x 17. Four factors, so composite. FALSE. E - 97: sqrt(97) is about 9.8, so test 2, 3, 5, 7 only. 97 is odd, 9 + 7 = 16 is not a multiple of 3, it does not end in 0 or 5, and 97/7 = 13.857... TRUE. Answer: B, C, E.

Trap. Selecting A because 1 'has no factors other than 1 and itself' - 1 is not prime, because it has only ONE factor, not two. The other bait is D: 51 is odd with no small factor in sight, but the digit sum 6 gives away the 3, and 51 = 3 x 17.

Q2 · NUM-PRF-003 — Answer: B

Step 1 — build a factor tree: 60 = 6 x 10. Step 2 — break each branch down: 6 = 2 x 3 and 10 = 2 x 5. Step 3 — collect the leaves: 60 = 2 x 3 x 2 x 5 = 2^2 x 3 x 5. Every leaf is prime, so the tree is finished. Check: 2^2 x 3 x 5 = 4 x 15 = 60. Correct. Answer: B. 2^2 x 3 x 5.

Trap. Choosing C or D: 15 and 4 are genuine factors of 60, but 15 = 3 x 5 and 4 = 2^2 are not prime, so neither expression is a PRIME factorization. Choice A drops one of the two 2s and multiplies back to 30, not 60 - always multiply back before committing.

Q3 · NUM-PRF-012 — Answer: 7

Step 1 — factor tree: 84 = 4 x 21. Step 2 — break the branches: 4 = 2 x 2 and 21 = 3 x 7. Step 3 — prime factorization: 84 = 2^2 x 3 x 7. The distinct primes are 2, 3 and 7. Step 4 — the largest of these is 7. Check: 2^2 x 3 x 7 = 4 x 21 = 84. Correct. Answer: 7.

Trap. Answering 21, which is the largest 'leftover' in the factor tree. 21 = 3 x 7 is composite, so it is a factor of 84 but not a prime factor. Answering 42 makes the same mistake one level higher up the tree.

Q4 · NUM-PRF-009 — Answer: C

Q5 · NUM-PRF-014 — Answer: D

C - 14 = 2 x 7 and 21 = 3 x 7. Both contain 7, so GCD = 7. Not coprime. D - 8 = 2^3 and 35 = 5 x 7. No prime appears in both lists, so GCD = 1. Coprime. E - 6 = 2 x 3 and 18 = 2 x 3^2. Trap. Choosing C: 14 and 21 have no common factor that jumps out, but both are multiples of 7, so their GCD is 7. Coprime does not mean 'neither is prime' or 'one is odd' - the only test is whether the two prime factorizations share a prime at all.

Q7 · NUM-PRF-018 — Answer: 210

Q10 · NUM-PRF-027 — Answer: D

Step 1 — use the identity GCD(a, b) x LCM(a, b) = a x b. It holds because each prime contributes min(exponent) to the GCD and max(exponent) to the LCM, and min + max = the sum of the two exponents. Step 2 — a x b = 12 x 180 = 2160. Check with an actual pair: a = 36, b = 60. GCD(36, 60) = 12 and LCM(36, 60) = 180, and 36 x 60 = 2160. Correct. Answer: D. 2160.

Trap. Combining the two numbers with the wrong operation: 180 + 12 = 192 (choice C) or 180 - 12 = 168 (choice B). Choice E, 3600, comes from assuming a = b = 180; choice A, 144, from assuming a = b = 12. Neither pair actually has GCD 12 and LCM 180.

Q12 · NUM-PRF-031 — Answer: A, D, E

Q18 · NUM-PRF-044 — Answer: C

Step 1 — x and y are distinct primes, so xy has exactly (1+1)(1+1) = 4 positive factors: 1, x, y and xy. Step 2 — Column A = 1 + x + y + xy. This is the capsule's sum-of-factors rule read backwards: the sum of the factors of p^a x q^b is (1 + p + ... + p^a)(1 + q + ... + q^b), and with a = b = 1 the brackets are (1 + x)(1 + y). Trap. Forgetting that 1 is a factor and summing only x + y + xy, which falls one short of (x + 1)(y + 1) and points to B. Choosing D because x and y are unnamed: the identity holds for every pair of distinct primes, so nothing depends on which ones. Choosing A comes from double-counting the two prime factors when summing: writing the factor list as 1 + x + y + xy and then adding x and y a second time as 'the primes themselves', which gives 1 + 2x + 2y + xy and overshoots (x + 1)(y + 1) = 1 + x + y + xy by x + y.

Q20 · NUM-PRF-046 — Answer: A, B, D

The governing fact: a positive integer has an ODD number of factors exactly when it is a perfect square. Trap. Selecting C because n = 2 gives 4, which has 3 factors - one lucky case is not 'always' (n = 1 gives 2, which has 2 factors). Q28 · NUM-PRF-058 — Answer: E

Let v be the exponent of p in the prime factorization of a, so a = p^v x m with m not divisible by p. Then a^2 = p^(2v) x m^2, and the exponent of p in a^2 is 2v - always an EVEN number. Step 1 — the hypothesis p^3 | a^2 says 2v >= 3. Since 2v is an even integer, 2v >= 3 forces 2v >= 4, that is v >= 2. This is the whole question: the exponent inside a square cannot be odd, so it cannot stop at 3. Step 2 — statement I. v >= 2 means p^2 divides a. TRUE. Step 3 — statement II. 2v >= 4 means p^4 divides a^2. TRUE. Step 4 — statement III. v >= 2 >= 1, so p divides a. TRUE. Check with the tightest case, p = 2 and a = 4: a^2 = 16, and 2^3 = 8 divides 16, so the hypothesis holds. Then 4 | 4 (I), 16 | 16 (II) and 2 | 4 (III) all hold. Check that no smaller a works: a = 2 gives a^2 = 4, and 8 does not divide 4, so the hypothesis fails there. Answer: E. I, II and III.

Trap. Choosing D by stopping at the half-insight: seeing that the exponent in a^2 is even, so p^3 | a^2 upgrades to p^4 | a^2 (II), and that p | a follows (III), but never pushing the same parity argument back into a to get v >= 2 and statement I. Choosing B comes from reading 2v >= 3 as v >= 1.5 and rounding DOWN to v >= 1, which yields only III. Choosing C drops II by treating it as a claim about a rather than about a^2. Choosing A is the mirror of that: having done the real work and derived v >= 2, the student reads II and III as mere restatements of the same fact about a rather than as separate consequences that still have to be checked, and so selects only the statement he feels he actually proved, I.
