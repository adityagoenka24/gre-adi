Q1 · NUM-GCL-001 — Answer: C

Step 1 — prime factorise both. 12 = 2^2 x 3 and 18 = 2 x 3^2. Step 2 — the GCD takes the LOWEST power of each SHARED prime. The shared primes are 2 and 3. Lowest power of 2: 2^1. Lowest power of 3: 3^1. Step 3 — multiply: GCD = 2 x 3 = 6. Check: 12/6 = 2 and 18/6 = 3, both integers, and GCD(2, 3) = 1, so nothing larger than 6 can divide both. Answer: C, 6.

Trap. Choosing 36 (option E) computes the LCM, 2^2 x 3^2, by taking the highest power of each prime. A GCD can never exceed the smaller number, so 36 is impossible on sight. Choosing 3 (option B) stops at one shared prime and forgets the shared 2. Choosing 9 (option D) uses 3^2, but 12 contains only one 3.

Q2 · NUM-GCL-011 — Answer: 18

Step 1 — 'when do the two cycles next coincide' is an LCM question, not a GCD question. Step 2 — prime factorise: 6 = 2 x 3 and 9 = 3^2. Step 3 — the LCM takes the highest power of every prime present: 2^1 x 3^2 = 18. Check: Bus A departs at 6, 12, 18; Bus B departs at 9, 18. The first shared minute is 18. Answer: 18.

Trap. Answering 3, which is GCD(6, 9). GCD answers 'how large can the equal groups be'; LCM answers 'when do the cycles line up again'. Answering 54 multiplies 6 x 9: that is a common multiple, but not the least one, because 6 and 9 already share a factor of 3.

Q3 · NUM-GCL-012 — Answer: C

Step 1 — LCM(a, b) is by definition a multiple of b. If that LCM equals a, then a is a multiple of b. Step 2 — 'a is a multiple of b' is the same statement as 'b divides a', so C is forced. Step 3 — test the others on a = 12, b = 6, where LCM(12, 6) = 12 = a. Here a is not equal to b, so A fails; a does not divide b, so D fails; GCD(12, 6) = 6, not 1, so E fails. Step 4 — B fails on a = b = 6, which also satisfies LCM(a, b) = a but has a not greater than b. Check: when b divides a, the multiples of b already contain a, so the least common multiple is a itself. Answer: C.

Trap. Picking A (a = b) or B (a > b): both describe cases that satisfy the condition, but neither is forced, since a = 12, b = 6 kills A and a = b = 6 kills B. Picking D reverses the divisibility - if a divided b the LCM would be b, not a.

Q5 · NUM-GCL-014 — Answer: C

Q8 · NUM-GCL-026 — Answer: C

Q9 · NUM-GCL-019 — Answer: C

Step 3 — the only positive integer that divides 1 is 1, so d = 1. Step 4 — this argument uses nothing about n, so the columns are equal for every positive integer n. Q10 · NUM-GCL-035 — Answer: C

Step 1 — any common divisor of m and n also divides m - n, so it is a common divisor of m - n and n. Step 2 — conversely, any common divisor of m - n and n also divides (m - n) + n = m, so it is a common divisor of m and n. Step 3 — the two pairs have exactly the same set of common divisors, hence the same greatest one. The columns are equal. Step 4 — this is the subtraction form of Euclid's algorithm, which is why GCD(a, b) = GCD(b, a mod b) works. Check: m = 48, n = 18 gives GCD(48, 18) = 6 and GCD(30, 18) = 6. Again with m = 35, n = 14: GCD(35, 14) = 7 and GCD(21, 14) = 7. Answer: C.

Trap. Answering A because m is bigger than m - n, assuming a larger number produces a larger GCD. Size is irrelevant: subtracting n removes nothing that m and n share. Answering D because m and n are unspecified misses that the identity holds for every such pair.

Q25 · NUM-GCL-050 — Answer: A, B, C
