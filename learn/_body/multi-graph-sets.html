2. Must-Know Rules

The standard combinations and what each supplies:

Combination Typical roles
Pie + bar pie gives shares, bar gives the totals to multiply by
Table + line graph table gives absolute values, line gives the trend
Two bar charts one gives volume, the other gives price or rate
Stacked bar + pie stacked gives composition over time, pie gives one year in detail

The three-step method:

  1. Identify what the question wants — an absolute value, a percent, a ratio, or a change.
  2. Decide which display holds each ingredient. Write them down separately.
  3. Combine, usually by multiplication (share x total) or division (value / value).

The key conversion: absolute value = share (from the pie) x total (from the bar or table)

Percent change from graphs. When a question asks for percent change and both years' values must be built from two displays, compute both absolute values first, then apply (new - old)/old. Do not try to combine percent changes of the share and the total in your head.

Though it is worth knowing: if the share stays constant, the category's percent change equals the total's percent change. If both change, the combined multiplier is (share multiplier) x (total multiplier).

3. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Pie plus bar

A bar chart shows total revenue: 2023 = $40m, 2024 = $50m. A pie chart shows 2024 revenue by division: Retail 35%, Wholesale 45%, Online 20%. What was 2024 Online revenue?

0.20 x 50 = $10 million.

Note the pie applies to 2024 only — you cannot use it for 2023 unless the problem says the shares were unchanged.

Example 2 — Combining two multipliers

A category's share of the total rises from 20% to 25%, while the total rises from $80m to $96m. By what percent did the category grow?

Old value: 0.20 x 80 = $16m.

New value: 0.25 x 96 = $24m.

Change = 8/16 = 50% increase.

(Check via multipliers: share x1.25, total x1.2, so 1.25 x 1.2 = 1.5. Same answer.)

Example 3 — Table plus graph

A table gives units sold per year; a line graph gives the price per unit. Units: 2023 = 12,000; 2024 = 15,000. Price: 2023 = $8; 2024 = $7. In which year was revenue higher?

2023: 12,000 x 8 = $96,000.

2024: 15,000 x 7 = $105,000.

2024 was higher, despite the lower price.

4. GRE Traps

  • Applying one year's pie chart to a different year. Shares change unless stated otherwise.
  • Adding percent changes. A 25% share rise and a 20% total rise is a 50% rise, not 45%.
  • Using the wrong total. With two bar charts present, check which one the question's category belongs to.
  • Mixing units across displays. One chart in thousands, another in millions.
  • Answering from one display when both are needed — the classic design of these sets.
  • Assuming a relationship the data does not state, such as constant shares or linear growth.
  • Reading the legend of the wrong chart.

5. Speed Tricks

  • Label each display in the margin with what it supplies: "shares" and "totals". Do this once for the whole set.
  • Convert shares to absolute values once, then answer all three questions from your converted numbers.
  • Multipliers compose: to combine a share change and a total change, multiply the multipliers.
  • Check the answer spread first. Multi-display questions often have widely separated choices, meaning estimation suffices.
  • Write intermediate values down. Holding four numbers from two charts in your head is where errors happen.
  • If a question feels unanswerable, you are probably missing the second display.

6. Self-Check

Q1. A pie shows a division at 30% of a total of $60m. What is the division's revenue?

Q2. A category's share rises from 10% to 12% while the total rises from 500 to 550. What is the percent increase in the category?

Q3. Units sold: 2,000 at $15 each in year 1; 2,500 at $13 each in year 2. Which year had higher revenue?

Answers

A1. 0.30 x 60 = $18 million.

A2. Old: 50. New: 66. Increase = 16/50 = 32%. (Or 1.2 x 1.1 = 1.32.)

A3. Year 1: 30,000. Year 2: 32,500. Year 2.

7. One-Line Summary for the Board

One display gives the share, the other gives the total — multiply. Never add percent changes; multiply the multipliers.
