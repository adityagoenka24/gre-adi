Part C — Worked Solutions

Q1 · NUM-REM-004 — Answer: C

Step 1 — translate the remainder. A remainder of 0 means 6 divides n exactly, so n = 6k for some integer k. Step 2 — test I. n = 6k = 2(3k), so n is a multiple of 2. True. Step 3 — test II. n = 6k = 3(2k), so n is a multiple of 3. True. Step 4 — test III. Try the smallest case, n = 6. Then 6 divided by 12 gives quotient 0 and remainder 6, so 6 is not divisible by 12. One counterexample kills the statement. False. Only I and II must hold, so the answer is C.

Trap. Choosing E by assuming that a multiple of 6 must also be a multiple of 12, the next multiple of 6. Divisibility passes DOWN to the factors of the divisor (2 and 3), never UP to its multiples: n = 6 is the counterexample.

Q2 · NUM-REM-005 — Answer: 5

Step 1 — write the division algorithm: 53 = 8q + r with 0 <= r < 8. Step 2 — find the largest multiple of 8 that does not exceed 53: 8 x 6 = 48, while 8 x 7 = 56 is too big. So q = 6. Step 3 — r = 53 - 48 = 5. Check: 5 is between 0 and 7, so it is a legal remainder, and 8 x 6 + 5 = 53. Correct. Answer: 5.

Trap. Overshooting to 8 x 7 = 56 and reporting 56 - 53 = 3. The quotient must be the largest multiple that stays BELOW the number; going past it turns the remainder into a negative one (-3), which then gets reported as 3.

Q3 · NUM-REM-006 — Answer: B

Quantity A: 2n is even for every integer n, and an even number divided by 2 leaves nothing over. Quantity A = 0. Quantity B: 2n + 1 is odd for every integer n, and an odd number divided by 2 leaves 1. Quantity B = 1. Check with n = 3: 6 divided by 2 is 3 remainder 0; 7 divided by 2 is 3 remainder 1. And with n = 10: 20 leaves 0, 21 leaves 1. 1 > 0, so Quantity B is greater.

Trap. Treating Quantity A as 'no remainder' and therefore as blank or undefined, and choosing D. A remainder of 0 is a perfectly ordinary value - it is what divisibility looks like - so Quantity A is the number 0 and the comparison resolves.

Q4 · NUM-REM-007 — Answer: D

Step 1 — the units digit of a product depends only on the units digits of the factors, because every other digit contributes a multiple of 10. Step 2 — units digit of 7 is 7; units digit of 48 is 8. Step 3 — 7 x 8 = 56, which ends in 6. Check: 7 x 48 = 336, and 336 does end in 6. Correct. Answer: 6.

Trap. Multiplying 7 by the tens digit instead: 7 x 4 = 28, so answering 8, which is choice E - but the tens digit of 48 only ever feeds the tens column and higher, and the units digit of the answer is decided by 7 x 8 alone. Choice C, 5, is the units digit of 7 + 8 = 15, from adding the units digits instead of multiplying them, and choice B, 4, is the tens digit of 48 copied straight down. Choice A, 3, is the tens digit of the product 336, read off in place of the units digit.

Q5 · NUM-REM-011 — Answer: C

Step 1 — write n in its remainder form: n = 7q + 4. Step 2 — add 7: n + 7 = 7q + 4 + 7 = 7(q + 1) + 4. Step 3 — the expression is again (a multiple of 7) + 4, and 4 is less than 7, so the remainder is 4. Check with the smallest legal n, n = 4: 4 + 7 = 11, and 11 = 7 x 1 + 4, remainder 4. Try n = 18: 18 + 7 = 25 = 7 x 3 + 4, remainder 4 again. Answer: 4.

Trap. Adding 7 to the remainder as well, giving 4 + 7 = 11, which is choice E; adding the divisor itself pushes the quotient up by 1 and leaves the remainder untouched. Choice D, 7, is the same instinct in a milder form - treating the added 7 as a fresh leftover chunk - and both 7 and 11 are impossible on their face, since a remainder on division by 7 must be 0 through 6. Choice B, 3, is the complement error 7 - 4 = 3, reporting what is left of the divisor instead of what is left of n, and choice A, 0, comes from believing that adding 7 completes the division and makes n + 7 divisible.

Q6 · NUM-REM-014 — Answer: C

Step 1 — use the product rule: the remainder of a product is the remainder of the product of the remainders, reduced again. Step 2 — rem(4n) = rem(4 x 5) = rem(20). Step 3 — reduce: 20 = 8 x 2 + 4, so the remainder is 4. The algebra behind it: n = 8k + 5, so 4n = 32k + 20 = 32k + 16 + 4 = 8(4k + 2) + 4. Check with n = 5: 4n = 20, and 20 = 8 x 2 + 4. Check with n = 13: 4n = 52 = 8 x 6 + 4. Same remainder both times. Answer: 4.

Trap. Stopping at 4 x 5 = 20 and choosing E. A remainder on division by 8 must be less than 8, so 20 is not finished - subtract 8 twice to reach 4. Choice D is the other live error: assuming that multiplying n leaves its remainder alone, the way ADDING a multiple of 8 would.

Q7 · NUM-REM-015 — Answer: E

Step 1 — build the cycle of powers of 2 on division by 5, reducing at every step: 2^1 = 2 2^2 = 4 2^3 = 8, which leaves 3 2^4 = 16, which leaves 1 2^5 leaves 2 again, so the cycle is (2, 4, 3, 1) and its length is 4. Step 2 — locate the exponent: 10 = 4 x 2 + 2, so 10 leaves remainder 2 on division by 4. Step 3 — take the 2nd entry of the cycle: 4. Check: 2^10 = 1024 = 5 x 204 + 4. Correct. Answer: 4.

Trap. Using a cycle length of 5 because there are 5 possible remainders, computing 10 leaves remainder 0 on division by 5, and landing on 1 (choice B). The length of the cycle is however many powers it takes to return to the start - here 4, not 5. Choice C is position 1 of the cycle and choice D is position 3, the two off-by-one slips on either side of the correct position 2; choice A is the belief that a power of 2 must eventually be divisible by 5.

Q8 · NUM-REM-017 — Answer: B

Reduce first, multiply or add second, then reduce again. Quantity A: rem(mn) = rem(4 x 3) = rem(12) = 0, since 12 = 6 x 2. Quantity B: rem(m + n) = rem(4 + 3) = rem(7) = 1, since 7 = 6 x 1 + 1. Check with actual numbers: m = 10, n = 9. Then mn = 90 = 6 x 15, remainder 0; m + n = 19 = 6 x 3 + 1, remainder 1. Try m = 16, n = 15: mn = 240 = 6 x 40, remainder 0; m + n = 31 = 6 x 5 + 1, remainder 1. The two remainders never move. 1 > 0, so Quantity B is greater.

Trap. Comparing 4 x 3 = 12 against 4 + 3 = 7, seeing 12 > 7, and choosing A. Neither number is a remainder yet: both must be take the remainderd on division by 6, and that reverses the comparison to 0 versus 1. The bigger intermediate product does not mean the bigger remainder.

Q9 · NUM-REM-019 — Answer: A, B, D

Every valid n has the form 12k + 5 with k an integer: 5, 17, 29, 41, 53, ... A - 5 = 12 x 0 + 5, remainder 5. True. B - 17 = 12 x 1 + 5, remainder 5. True. C - 24 = 12 x 2 + 0, remainder 0. False. D - 29 = 12 x 2 + 5, remainder 5. True. E - 37 = 12 x 3 + 1, remainder 1. False. Answer: A, B, D.

Trap. Rejecting A because 5 is smaller than the divisor. k = 0 is allowed, so a number smaller than the divisor simply IS its own remainder: 5 divided by 12 gives quotient 0 and remainder 5. The other bait is C: 24 is a multiple of 12, which looks like it belongs to the 12-pattern, but its remainder is 0, not 5.

Q10 · NUM-REM-023 — Answer: A

Step 1 — take the remainder on division by 9. The remainder of k is 7. For 20: 20 = 9 x 2 + 2, so its remainder is 2. Step 2 — add the remainders: 7 + 2 = 9. Step 3 — reduce again: 9 = 9 x 1 + 0, so the remainder is 0. Check with the smallest k, k = 7: 7 + 20 = 27 = 9 x 3, remainder 0. Try k = 16: 16 + 20 = 36 = 9 x 4, remainder 0. Answer: 0.

Trap. Stopping at 7 + 2 = 9 and choosing D. A remainder on division by 9 has to be less than 9, so 9 must be reduced one more time to 0. Choice E is the same failure one step earlier (7 + 20 = 27 left unreduced), choice C ignores the +20 as if 20 were a multiple of 9, and choice B keeps only the 20's remainder and forgets k's.

Q11 · NUM-REM-029 — Answer: B

Step 1 — write the form: m = 14q + 9. Step 2 — since 7 divides 14, the term 14q is already a multiple of 7. Only the 9 can contribute. Step 3 — reduce the 9: 9 = 7 x 1 + 2, so m = 7(2q + 1) + 2 and the remainder is 2. Check with the smallest m, m = 9: 9 = 7 + 2, remainder 2. Try m = 23: 23 = 7 x 3 + 2, remainder 2. Try m = 37: 37 = 7 x 5 + 2, remainder 2. Answer: 2. The general rule: when the new divisor is a FACTOR of the old one, just reduce the old remainder by the new divisor.

Trap. Choosing E and carrying the 9 across unchanged - impossible, since a remainder on division by 7 must be under 7. Choice D comes from splitting 9 = 7 + 2 and reporting the 7 rather than the leftover 2; choice A comes from assuming that because 7 divides 14, it must divide m as well.

Q12 · NUM-REM-030 — Answer: A, B, C

Write n = 8k + 3 and test each statement. A - 8k is even and 3 is odd, so n is odd for every k. True. B - 4 divides 8, so 8k is a multiple of 4 and only the 3 survives: n = 4(2k) + 3, remainder 3 (and 3 < 4, so it is already reduced). True. C - rem(n^2) = rem(3 x 3) = rem(9) = 1. Check: n = 3 gives 9 = 8 + 1; n = 11 gives 121 = 8 x 15 + 1. True. D - 16 is a MULTIPLE of 8, not a factor, so the remainder does not carry over. n = 3 leaves 3 on division by 16, but n = 11 leaves 11 on division by 16. False. E - rem(3n) = rem(3 x 3) = rem(9) = 1, not 9; and 9 cannot be a remainder on division by 8 at all. False. Answer: A, B, C.

Trap. D and E are the two halves of the same habit. D assumes a remainder survives a jump to a MULTIPLE of the divisor (it only survives a jump to a factor, which is why B is true), and E leaves 3 x 3 = 9 unreduced even though every remainder on division by 8 must be under 8.

Q13 · NUM-REM-031 — Answer: 3

Step 1 — handle the huge term first. 50! is the product of every integer from 1 to 50, and 7 is one of those factors, so 7 divides 50! Exactly. Step 2 — therefore rem(50!) = 0. There is nothing to compute and no cycle to find. Step 3 — apply the sum rule: rem(50! + 3) = rem(0 + 3) = 3, and 3 < 7, so it is already reduced. Answer: 3. The general fact: n! is divisible by every integer from 1 to n, so rem(n!) = 0 for any divisor d <= n.

Trap. Trying to attack 50! itself - hunting for a cycle in its digits or estimating its size. Nothing needs to be computed: 7 is one of the factors being multiplied, so the remainder is 0 and only the +3 is left. Students who instead reduce 50 leaves remainder 1 on division by 7 and treat the term as 1 answer 4.

Q14 · NUM-REM-034 — Answer: C

Step 1 — build the cycle of powers of 2 on division by 7, reducing at each step: 2^1 = 2 2^2 = 4 2^3 = 8, which leaves 1 2^4 leaves 2 again. The cycle is (2, 4, 1) with length 3 - short, because 2^3 = 8 is just one more than 7. Step 2 — locate the exponent: 100 = 3 x 33 + 1, so 100 leaves remainder 1 on division by 3. Step 3 — take the 1st entry of the cycle: 2. Check by rebuilding: 2^100 = 2^99 x 2 = (2^3)^33 x 2, and 2^3 leaves 1, so (1)^33 x 2 = 2. Answer: 2.

Trap. Dividing the exponent by the DIVISOR instead of by the cycle length: 100 leaves remainder 2 on division by 7, then 2^2 = 4, which is choice E. The exponent is always divided by the length of the cycle, here 3, and that remainder locates the entry. Choice B comes from reading position 1 off a cycle started at 2^0 = 1 rather than 2^1; choice D reports the cycle LENGTH (3) instead of the entry sitting there; and choice A assumes that a high enough power of 2 must eventually be divisible by 7 - it never is, since 7 is prime and does not divide 2, so remainder 0 is unreachable here.

Q15 · NUM-REM-035 — Answer: 7

Write n = 8q + 5 for some integer q. 3n = 24q + 15 = 24q + 8 + 7 = 8(3q + 1) + 7. The remainder is 7. Check with n = 13: 3n = 39 = 8 * 4 + 7.

Trap. Multiplying the remainder by 3 and entering 15. A remainder on division by 8 must be less than 8: 15 = 8 + 7, so the remainder is 7.

Q16 · NUM-REM-036 — Answer: C

Handle each power separately, then add and reduce. Step 1 — powers of 3 on division by 5: 3, then 9 leaves 4, then 12 leaves 2, then 6 leaves 1. The cycle is (3, 4, 2, 1), length 4. 100 leaves remainder 0 on division by 4, and when the exponent lands on 0 you take the LAST entry of the cycle, not a nonexistent 0th one. So 3^100 leaves 1. Step 2 — powers of 4 on division by 5: 4, then 16 leaves 1. The cycle is (4, 1), length 2. 100 leaves remainder 0 on division by 2, so again the last entry: 4^100 leaves 1. Faster for 4: 4 is one less than 5, so 4 behaves like -1. Then 4^100 = (-1)^100 = 1. Even exponent, remainder 1; odd exponent, remainder 4. Step 3 — add the remainders: 1 + 1 = 2, and 2 < 5, so the remainder is 2. Answer: 2.

Trap. The off-by-one on the cycle. With 100 leaves remainder 0 on division by 4, a cycle position of 0 always means the END of the cycle - the 4th entry, 1 - but students read it as a nonexistent 0th position. Reading it as the remainder 0 for both terms gives choice A; reading position 1 for 3^100 (that is, 3) while handling 4^100 correctly gives 3 + 1 = 4, choice E; and reading position 1 for 3^100 while calling 4^100 zero gives choice D. Choice B is what you get by evaluating 3^100 and then forgetting the second term altogether.

Q17 · NUM-REM-041 — Answer: A

Fastest route - factor before you divide. 5^n + 5^(n+1) = 5^n(1 + 5) = 6 x 5^n, which is a multiple of 6 for every n. The remainder is 0. The cyclicity route, for confirmation: 5 is one less than 6, so 5 behaves like -1. Powers of 5 on division by 6 therefore cycle (5, 1) with length 2. If n is odd: 5^n leaves 5 and 5^(n+1) leaves 1, so the sum leaves 5 + 1 = 6, which reduces to 0. If n is even: 5^n leaves 1 and 5^(n+1) leaves 5, so the sum leaves 1 + 5 = 6, which reduces to 0. Either way the two consecutive cycle entries add to exactly the divisor. Check: n = 1 gives 5 + 25 = 30 = 6 x 5. n = 2 gives 25 + 125 = 150 = 6 x 25. n = 3 gives 125 + 625 = 750 = 6 x 125. Answer: 0.

Trap. Adding the two cycle entries to get 6 and choosing D - but 6 is not a legal remainder on division by 6, and reducing once more gives 0. Choice E is the other bait: the two cases really do look different (5 + 1 versus 1 + 5), yet both sum to 6, so the parity of n never reaches the answer.

Q18 · NUM-REM-043 — Answer: D

Write n = 9q + 4. n^2 = 81q^2 + 72q + 16 = 9(9q^2 + 8q + 1) + 7. The remainder is 7. Check with n = 13: 169 = 9 * 18 + 7. Answer: D.

Trap. Squaring the remainder to get 16 and then keeping 4 (choice B) or doubling to get 8 (choice E). 16 is larger than 9, so divide again: 16 = 9 + 7.

Q19 · NUM-REM-044 — Answer: A

Step 1 — Quantity A: 6t is a multiple of 6 whatever t is, so 6t + 5 is already in the form 6(t) + 5 with 5 < 6. The remainder is exactly 5, always. Step 2 — Quantity B: take the remainder on division by 5. 6 leaves 1, so 6t leaves the same as t; and 5 leaves 0. So the remainder of 6t + 5 on division by 5 equals the remainder of t on division by 5, which is 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4 depending on t. Step 3 — compare. Quantity B is at most 4, because any remainder on division by 5 is under 5. Quantity A is fixed at 5. So Quantity A > Quantity B for every t. Check: t = 1 gives 11 - remainders 5 and 1. t = 4 gives 29 - remainders 5 and 4. t = 5 gives 35 - remainders 5 and 0. Quantity A wins every time. Quantity A is greater.

Trap. Choosing D because Quantity B moves as t changes. It moves, but it is trapped below 5 by the definition of a remainder, while Quantity A is pinned at 5 - a varying quantity can still be decisively smaller. The other slip is computing Quantity B at t = 5, getting 0, and assuming Quantity A must also collapse to 0 there; 35 divided by 6 still leaves 5.

Q20 · NUM-REM-047 — Answer: D

The days of the week repeat every 7 days. 100 = 7 * 14 + 2, so 100 days from today is the same day of the week as 2 days from today. Two days after Tuesday is Thursday. Answer: D.

Trap. Dividing 100 by 7 and using the quotient 14 in place of the remainder, or counting Tuesday itself as day 1 and landing on Wednesday (choice C). Only the remainder, 2, matters, and the count starts the day after today.

Q21 · NUM-REM-037 — Answer: B

Work with the remainder of n, which is 2, and reduce at each step. Step 1 — rem(n^2) = rem(2 x 2) = rem(4) = 1. Step 2 — Quantity A: rem(n^2 + n) = rem(1 + 2) = rem(3) = 0. Step 3 — Quantity B: rem(n^2 - n) = rem(1 - 2) = rem(-1). A negative value is not a legal remainder, so add the divisor: -1 + 3 = 2. Check with n = 2: n^2 + n = 6, remainder 0; n^2 - n = 2, remainder 2. Check with n = 5: 30 leaves 0, and 20 = 3 x 6 + 2 leaves 2. Check with n = 8: 72 leaves 0, and 56 = 3 x 18 + 2 leaves 2. The values never move. 0 < 2, so Quantity B is greater.

Trap. Two failures compete. One is stopping at rem(-1) and calling Quantity B negative, hence smaller than 0 - the GRE only uses remainders in the range 0 to d - 1, so -1 becomes 2. The other is testing n = 2, getting 0 and 2, then choosing D out of doubt that other values of n might behave differently. Since only the remainder of n enters the calculation and that remainder is fixed at 2, both columns are locked.

Q22 · NUM-REM-038 — Answer: A, C, E

Step 1 — characterise the numbers first. Let the common remainder be r. Then n - r is a multiple of 4 and also a multiple of 6, so n - r is a multiple of the least common multiple of 4 and 6 = 12, giving n = 12k + r. Step 2 — bound r. It must be a legal remainder for both divisors, so r < 4: r is 0, 1, 2 or 3. So n works exactly when n leaves 0, 1, 2 or 3 on division by 12. A - 14 = 12 + 2, so it qualifies. Confirm: 14 = 4 x 3 + 2 and 14 = 6 x 2 + 2. Both remainders are 2. True. B - 20 = 12 + 8, and 8 is not in {0, 1, 2, 3}. Confirm: 20 leaves 0 on division by 4 but 2 on division by 6. False. C - 25 = 24 + 1, so it qualifies. Confirm: 25 = 4 x 6 + 1 and 25 = 6 x 4 + 1. Both remainders are 1. True. D - 30 = 24 + 6, and 6 is not in {0, 1, 2, 3}. Confirm: 30 leaves 2 on division by 4 but 0 on division by 6. False. E - 39 = 36 + 3, so it qualifies. Confirm: 39 = 4 x 9 + 3 and 39 = 6 x 6 + 3. Both remainders are 3. True. Answer: A, C, E.

Trap. Choosing B and D because they are 'divisible by one of the divisors' and so look regular: 20 is a multiple of 4 and 30 is a multiple of 6, but a shared remainder needs both divisions to agree, and each of these leaves 0 on one side and 2 on the other. The one-step test is n on division by 12: only 0, 1, 2 and 3 survive, because the common remainder must be smaller than the smaller divisor.

Q23 · NUM-REM-040 — Answer: C

Step 1 — describe every prime above 3. Such a p is divisible by neither 2 nor 3, so on division by 6 it cannot leave 0, 2, 3 or 4. Only remainders 1 and 5 are left, i.e. p = 6k + 1 or p = 6k - 1. Step 2 — square that form: p^2 = (6k +/- 1)^2 = 36k^2 +/- 12k + 1 = 12(3k^2 +/- k) + 1. Step 3 — the bracket is an integer, so p^2 is a multiple of 12 plus 1, and 1 < 12 is already reduced. The remainder is 1 for every prime p > 3. Check: 5^2 = 25 = 12 x 2 + 1. 7^2 = 49 = 12 x 4 + 1. 11^2 = 121 = 12 x 10 + 1. 13^2 = 169 = 12 x 14 + 1. Quantity A = 1 = Quantity B, so the two quantities are equal.

Trap. Testing two or three primes, getting 1 every time, and still choosing D because 'p could be any prime'. The 6k +/- 1 form covers every prime above 3 at once, so the result is proved, not sampled. The stem's 'greater than 3' matters: p = 3 gives 9, and p = 2 gives 4, neither of which is 1.

Q24 · NUM-REM-042 — Answer: 9

Step 1 — find where the terms die. 12 = 4 x 3, so a factorial is divisible by 12 as soon as it contains both a 4 and a 3: 4! = 24 = 12 x 2 already does, and every later factorial contains 4! as a factor. So 4!, 5!, ..., 20! All leave remainder 0 and can be ignored entirely. Step 2 — only the first three terms survive: 1! + 2! + 3! = 1 + 2 + 6 = 9. Step 3 — reduce: 9 < 12, so the remainder is 9. Check: 1! + 2! + 3! + 4! = 33, and 33 = 12 x 2 + 9. Adding 5! = 120 = 12 x 10 changes nothing: 153 = 12 x 12 + 9. Correct. Answer: 9.

Trap. Cutting the sum off at the wrong place and answering 3. Students stop after 2! because 3! = 6 already contains 2 x 3, forgetting that 12 = 2 x 2 x 3 needs a SECOND factor of 2, which only arrives at 4! = 24. The cut-off is the first factorial containing every prime factor of the divisor to its full power - here 4!, not 3!.

Q25 · NUM-REM-045 — Answer: B, D

Q26 · NUM-REM-046 — Answer: 6

Method 1 - group the cycle. Step 1 — powers of 2 on division by 7 cycle (2, 4, 1) with length 3, since 2^3 = 8 leaves 1. Step 2 — each block of three consecutive powers contributes 2 + 4 + 1 = 7, which leaves 0. The blocks vanish. Step 3 — count: 62 = 3 x 20 + 2, so the first 60 terms form 20 complete blocks contributing 0, and two terms are left over: 2^61 and 2^62. Step 4 — 61 leaves remainder 1 on division by 3, so 2^61 leaves 2; 62 leaves remainder 2 on division by 3, so 2^62 leaves 4. Step 5 — total: 0 + 2 + 4 = 6. Method 2 - sum the geometric series first. 2^1 + 2^2 + ... + 2^62 = 2^63 - 2. 2^63 = (2^3)^21, and 2^3 leaves 1, so 2^63 leaves 1^21 = 1. So the sum leaves 1 - 2 = -1, and a negative remainder is completed by adding the divisor: -1 + 7 = 6. Both methods give 6. Answer: 6.

Trap. Assuming the blocks of three sweep up everything and answering 0. 62 is not a multiple of 3, so two powers hang off the end and they carry the whole answer. The second trap sits in Method 2: stopping at -1, which is not a legal remainder - add 7 to land in the range 0 to 6.

Q27 · NUM-REM-049 — Answer: 123

Q28 · NUM-REM-050 — Answer: B, D

Write n = 6q + 4 for an integer q >= 0, so n is 4, 10, 16, 22, ... A: 10 is not a multiple of 4. Not necessarily true. B: n = 6q + 4 = 3(2q + 1) + 1, so the remainder on division by 3 is 1. Must be true. C: 2n = 12q + 8 = 6(2q + 1) + 2, so the remainder is 2, never 4. False. D: n^2 = 36q^2 + 48q + 16 = 6(6q^2 + 8q + 2) + 4, so the remainder is 4. Must be true. E: n = 10 leaves remainder 10 when divided by 12. Not necessarily true. Check with n = 10: 10/3 leaves 1; 100 = 6 * 16 + 4. Answer: B, D.

Q29 · SEC-AO-008 — Answer: 6

A number and its remainder behave identically under squaring on division by 50, so reduce the base first. 871,434 = 50 x 17,428 + 34, so it leaves remainder 34. Then 34^2 = 1,156, and 1,156 = 50 x 23 + 6. The remainder is 6.

Trap. Students try to square 871,434 in full, a twelve-digit computation the GRE never intends, or they take the remainder on division by 50 only after squaring. Reducing the base to 34 before squaring turns the whole problem into 34^2.

Q30 · NUM-REM-048 — Answer: D

Quantity A is fixed. Factor: n^3 - n = n(n^2 - 1) = (n - 1)n(n + 1), the product of three consecutive integers. Among any three consecutive integers at least one is even and exactly one is a multiple of 3, so the product is divisible by 2 x 3 = 6. Quantity A = 0 for every n. Quantity B moves. Powers of 2 on division by 3 cycle (2, 1) with length 2, because 2 is one less than 3 and so behaves like -1: n odd -> 2^n leaves 2 -> 2^n + 1 leaves 2 + 1 = 3, which reduces to 0. n even -> 2^n leaves 1 -> 2^n + 1 leaves 1 + 1 = 2. Now compare: n = 1: Quantity A = 0 (0 divided by 6) and Quantity B = 0 (3 divided by 3). The columns are EQUAL. n = 2: Quantity A = 0 (n^3 - n = 6, and 6 divided by 6 leaves 0). Quantity B: 2^2 + 1 = 5, and 5 = 3 x 1 + 2, so Quantity B = 2. Quantity B is greater. n = 3: Quantity A = 0 (24 divided by 6) and Quantity B = 0 (9 divided by 3). Equal again. Two different relationships occur, so the relationship cannot be determined.

Trap. Testing only n = 1, where both columns happen to be 0, and choosing C. Quantity A is genuinely invariant, but Quantity B swings between 0 and 2 with the parity of n - and 'leaves remainder 3, so 0' at odd n is exactly the coincidence that makes n = 1 look conclusive. Whenever a column depends on n, test at least an odd and an even value before answering.
