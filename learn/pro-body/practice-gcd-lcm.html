Q1 · NUM-GCL-001 — Answer: C

Step 1 — prime factorize both. 12 = 2^2 x 3 and 18 = 2 x 3^2. Step 2 — the greatest common divisor takes the LOWEST power of each SHARED prime. The shared primes are 2 and 3. Lowest power of 2: 2^1. Lowest power of 3: 3^1. Step 3 — multiply: greatest common divisor = 2 x 3 = 6. Check: 12/6 = 2 and 18/6 = 3, both integers, and the greatest common divisor of 2 and 3 = 1, so nothing larger than 6 can divide both. Answer: C, 6.

Trap. Choosing 36 (option E) computes the least common multiple, 2^2 x 3^2, by taking the highest power of each prime. A greatest common divisor can never exceed the smaller number, so 36 is impossible on sight. Choosing 3 (option B) stops at one shared prime and forgets the shared 2. Choosing 9 (option D) uses 3^2, but 12 contains only one 3.

Q2 · NUM-GCL-011 — Answer: 18

Step 1 — 'when do the two cycles next coincide' is an least common multiple question, not a greatest common divisor question. Step 2 — prime factorize: 6 = 2 x 3 and 9 = 3^2. Step 3 — the least common multiple takes the highest power of every prime present: 2^1 x 3^2 = 18. Check: Bus A departs at 6, 12, 18; Bus B departs at 9, 18. The first shared minute is 18. Answer: 18.

Trap. Answering 3, which is the greatest common divisor of 6 and 9. Greatest common divisor answers 'how large can the equal groups be'; least common multiple answers 'when do the cycles line up again'. Answering 54 multiplies 6 x 9: that is a common multiple, but not the least one, because 6 and 9 already share a factor of 3.

Q3 · NUM-GCL-012 — Answer: C

Step 1 — the least common multiple of a and b is by definition a multiple of b. If that least common multiple equals a, then a is a multiple of b. Step 2 — 'a is a multiple of b' is the same statement as 'b divides a', so C is forced. Step 3 — test the others on a = 12, b = 6, where the least common multiple of 12 and 6 = 12 = a. Here a is not equal to b, so A fails; a does not divide b, so D fails; the greatest common divisor of 12 and 6 = 6, not 1, so E fails. Step 4 — B fails on a = b = 6, which also satisfies the least common multiple of a and b = a but has a not greater than b. Check: when b divides a, the multiples of b already contain a, so the least common multiple is a itself. Answer: C.

Trap. Picking A (a = b) or B (a > b): both describe cases that satisfy the condition, but neither is forced, since a = 12, b = 6 kills A and a = b = 6 kills B. Picking D reverses the divisibility - if a divided b the least common multiple would be b, not a.

Q5 · NUM-GCL-014 — Answer: C

Step 1 — distinct primes share no prime factor, so the greatest common divisor of p and q = 1. Step 2 — the least common multiple of p and q equals (p x q) divided by their greatest common divisor, so it is (p x q) / 1 = p x q. Step 3 — the columns are therefore equal for every choice of distinct primes. Check: p = 3, q = 5 gives a least common multiple of 15 and 3 x 5 = 15. p = 2, q = 7 gives a least common multiple of 14 and 2 x 7 = 14. Answer: C.

Trap. Answering D because p and q are unspecified. Coprimality alone fixes the relationship, whatever the primes are. Answering B comes from the reflex that a least common multiple is smaller than the product - true only when the two numbers share a factor, which distinct primes never do.

Q6 · NUM-GCL-019 — Answer: C

Step 1 — let d = the greatest common divisor of n and n + 1. Then d divides n and d divides n + 1. Step 2 — anything dividing both also divides their difference: (n + 1) - n = 1. Step 3 — the only positive integer that divides 1 is 1, so d = 1. Step 4 — this argument uses nothing about n, so the columns are equal for every positive integer n. Check: the greatest common divisor of 8 and 9 = 1, the greatest common divisor of 35 and 36 = 1, the greatest common divisor of 100 and 101 = 1. Answer: C.

Trap. Answering A for large n, reasoning that big numbers must share big factors. Consecutive integers are always coprime; one of them being even does not help, because the other is then odd. Answering D because n is unspecified misses that the difference argument pins d to 1 for every n.

Q9 · NUM-GCL-026 — Answer: C

Step 2 — the least common multiple of a and b = 4 x m x n, so maximizing the least common multiple means maximizing m x n over coprime pairs with m, n <= 5. Step 3 — the largest such product is m = 4, n = 5, since the greatest common divisor of 4 and 5 = 1 and 4 x 5 = 20. The pair m = 5, n = 5 is not allowed because the greatest common divisor of 5 and 5 = 5. Step 4 — least common multiple = 4 x 20 = 80, reached at a = 16 and b = 20. Trap. Choosing 60 (option B) stops at the coprime pair m = 3, n = 5 and misses that m = 4, n = 5 is also coprime. Q12 · NUM-GCL-033 — Answer: A, B, D

A - the least common multiple is a multiple of a, and the smallest positive multiple of a is a itself, so the least common multiple of a and b >= a. True. B - a x b is always a common multiple of a and b, and the least common multiple is the LEAST common multiple, so it cannot exceed a x b. True. C - equality holds only when the greatest common divisor of a and b = 1. Take a = 4, b = 6: least common multiple = 12 but a x b = 24. False. D - the greatest common divisor of a and b divides a, and a divides the least common multiple of a and b, so the greatest common divisor divides the least common multiple. True. E - the inequality is strict, and that fails when a = b: the greatest common divisor of 6 and 6 = 6 and the least common multiple of 6 and 6 = 6, equal, not less. False. Check on a = 4, b = 6: greatest common divisor = 2, least common multiple = 12. Indeed 12 >= 4, 12 <= 24, and 12 is a multiple of 2. Answer: A, B, D.

Trap. Selecting C because least common multiple = a x b is the case students meet most often - it is exactly the coprime case, and it breaks the moment a and b share a factor, as 4 and 6 do. Selecting E is the 'greatest common divisor is always smaller than least common multiple' reflex; the two coincide precisely when a = b.

Q13 · NUM-GCL-035 — Answer: C

Conversely, any common divisor of m - n and n also divides (m - n) + n = m, so it is a common divisor of m and n. The two pairs have exactly the same common divisors, hence the same greatest one, so the two quantities are equal. Check: m = 48, n = 18 gives 6 for both (48 and 18; 30 and 18). With m = 35, n = 14, both are 7. Answer: C.

Trap. Answering A because m is larger than m - n, assuming a larger number gives a larger greatest common divisor; subtracting n removes nothing that m and n share. Answering D because m and n are not specified misses that the identity holds for every such pair.

Q15 · NUM-GCL-053 — Answer: B

A. 80 = (2^4)(5) and 100 = (2^2)(5^2), so the least common multiple is (2^4)(5^2) = 400. The multiples below 2,000 are 400, 800, 1,200, and 1,600: four possibilities. B. 120 = (2^3)(3)(5), so the least common multiple is (2^4)(3)(5) = 240, giving 240 through 1,920: eight possibilities. C. 125 = 5^3, so the least common multiple is (2^4)(5^3) = 2,000, and no multiple of 2,000 is less than 2,000: no possibility at all. D. 150 = (2)(3)(5^2), so the least common multiple is (2^4)(3)(5^2) = 1,200. The only multiple below 2,000 is 1,200 itself: exactly one possibility. E. 180 = (2^2)(3^2)(5), so the least common multiple is (2^4)(3^2)(5) = 720, giving 720 and 1,440: two possibilities. Only choice D pins the number down.

Trap. Students see that 80 and 125 share only a factor of 5 and that their least common multiple, 2,000, sits right at the stated bound, and read that as fixing the count, choosing C. No multiple of 2,000 is less than 2,000, so that statement describes no collection at all and determines nothing.

Q30 · NUM-GCL-058 — Answer: 16

Step 1 — write a = 6m and b = 6n where m and n have greatest common divisor 1. Step 2 — then the least common multiple of a and b = 6 x m x n = 1260, so m x n = 210. Step 3 — 210 = 2 x 3 x 5 x 7: four distinct primes, each to the first power. Step 4 — because m and n must be coprime, each prime goes entirely to m or entirely to n. That is an independent 2-way choice for each of the 4 primes. Step 5 — the number of ordered pairs is 2^4 = 16. Check one concrete case: m = 2 x 3 = 6 and n = 5 x 7 = 35 give a = 36 and b = 210. The greatest common divisor of 36 and 210 = 6 and the least common multiple of 36 and 210 = 1260. Correct. And the identity holds: 6 x 1260 = 7560 = 36 x 210. Answer: 16.

Trap. Answering 8 by counting unordered pairs when the question asks for ordered ones. Answering 4 assumes the primes 2 and 3 are fully spent on the greatest common divisor so only 5 and 7 are free to move; but 1260/6 = 210 still contains one 2 and one 3, and each of those must also be assigned to m or to n. Working with 1260's exponents directly, without first reducing to m x n = 210, loses the coprimality that makes every choice binary.
