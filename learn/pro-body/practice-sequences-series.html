Part C — Worked Solutions

Q1 · ARI-SEQ-001 — Answer: C

Step 1 — identify a and d. The first term is a = 3, and each step adds d = 7 - 3 = 4. Step 2 — use a_n = a + (n - 1)d. Getting from term 1 to term 10 takes 9 steps, not 10. a_10 = 3 + 9 x 4 = 3 + 36 = 39. Check by listing: 3, 7, 11, 15, 19, 23, 27, 31, 35, 39. The tenth number is 39. Correct. Answer: 39.

Trap. Using a + nd instead of a + (n - 1)d: 3 + 10 x 4 = 43, choice E. The first term has had zero steps of d applied to it, so the 10th term has had 9. Choice A, 35, is the mirror slip of counting only 8 steps; choices B and D drop the first term and report just the accumulated difference, 9 x 4 = 36 and 10 x 4 = 40.

Q2 · ARI-SEQ-003 — Answer: C

Step 1 — check ratios, not differences: 6/2 = 3, 18/6 = 3, 54/18 = 3. So r = 3 and a = 2. Step 2 — use a_n = a x r^(n-1). The exponent is 4, not 5. a_5 = 2 x 3^4 = 2 x 81 = 162. Check by extending the list: 2, 6, 18, 54, 162. Correct. Answer: 162.

Trap. Writing 2 x 3^5 = 486, choice E - the same off-by-one as in arithmetic sequences, now living in the exponent. Choice A, 90, comes from treating the sequence as arithmetic and adding the last difference (54 + 36). Choice B, 108, doubles the 4th term instead of tripling it, and choice D, 324, multiplies 54 by the second term 6 rather than by the ratio.

Q3 · ARI-SEQ-007 — Answer: C

Step 1 — count the terms. The list is 2, 4, 6, ..., 20 with step 2, so the count is (20 - 2)/2 + 1 = 9 + 1 = 10 terms. Say the '+1' out loud. Step 2 — sum = count x average, and for an evenly spaced list the average is (first + last)/2 = (2 + 20)/2 = 11. Step 3 — sum = 10 x 11 = 110. Check: 2 + 20 = 22, 4 + 18 = 22, 6 + 16 = 22, 8 + 14 = 22, 10 + 12 = 22. Five pairs of 22 = 110. Correct. Answer: 110.

Trap. Stopping the count at (20 - 2)/2 = 9 terms, which gives 9 x 11 = 99 (choice A); overshooting to 11 terms gives 121 (choice D). Choice E, 210, is the sum of all integers from 1 to 20, and choice B, 100, is 10^2, the sum of the first 10 ODD numbers, not the even ones.

Q4 · ARI-SEQ-010 — Answer: B, D, E

A sequence is arithmetic when the DIFFERENCES between consecutive terms are constant. A - differences are 2, 4, 8: not constant. The ratios are constant (r = 2), so this one is geometric. False. B - differences are 4, 4, 4. Constant, so arithmetic with d = 4. True. C - differences are 3, 5, 7: not constant. These are the perfect squares. False. D - differences are -3, -3, -3. Constant, so arithmetic with d = -3. A negative common difference is still a common difference. True. E - differences are 0, 0, 0. Constant, so arithmetic with d = 0. True. Answer: B, D, E.

Trap. Two errors sit on opposite sides. Including A, because 2, 4, 8, 16 'has a pattern' - it does, but a multiplicative one. Excluding D or E, because the differences are not positive: d = -3 and d = 0 are perfectly legal common differences.

Q5 · ARI-SEQ-015 — Answer: 18

Step 1 — the step size is d = 8 - 5 = 3, first term 5, last term 56. Step 2 — count = (last - first)/step + 1 = (56 - 5)/3 + 1 = 51/3 + 1 = 17 + 1 = 18. Step 3 — why the +1: 51/3 = 17 counts the GAPS between terms, and a row of 17 gaps has 18 terms at its ends. Check with the nth-term formula: 56 = 5 + (n - 1)3 gives (n - 1)3 = 51, n - 1 = 17, n = 18. Correct. Answer: 18.

Trap. Answering 17, the number of steps of 3 needed to walk from 5 up to 56. Fence posts, not fence panels: from 5 to 56 there are 17 jumps but 18 numbers. This is the single most common error in the whole topic.

Q6 · ARI-SEQ-014 — Answer: D

Step 1 — one block has length 3 and sums to 4 + 9 + 2 = 15. Step 2 — divide the position count by the cycle length: 50 = 3 x 16 + 2. So there are 16 complete blocks (48 terms) with 2 terms left over. Step 3 — the complete blocks contribute 16 x 15 = 240. Step 4 — the remainder is 2, so the leftover terms are the FIRST two of a fresh block: term 49 = 4 and term 50 = 9, contributing 4 + 9 = 13. Step 5 — total = 240 + 13 = 253. Check: term 48 is the last of block 16, so term 49 restarts at 4. Correct. Answer: 253.

Trap. Stopping at 240 (choice A), the 16 complete blocks, and forgetting the two terms hanging off the end. Choice E, 255, is the opposite error of rounding 50/3 up to 17 whole blocks, which counts 51 terms. Choice C, 251, takes the leftover terms from the wrong end of the block (9 + 2 instead of 4 + 9), and choice B, 244, counts only one leftover term instead of two. In a cycle question the remainder tells you how far INTO the next block you land, counting from its start.

Q7 · ARI-SEQ-017 — Answer: 59

Step 1 — count the steps between the known terms: from position 3 to position 7 is 7 - 3 = 4 steps, not 5 and not 7. Step 2 — those 4 steps produce a rise of 27 - 11 = 16, so d = 16/4 = 4. Step 3 — jump straight from the 7th term to the 15th: that is 15 - 7 = 8 more steps. a_15 = 27 + 8 x 4 = 27 + 32 = 59. Check by going back to the first term instead: a_3 = 11 means a_1 = 11 - 2 x 4 = 3, and a_15 = 3 + 14 x 4 = 3 + 56 = 59. Both routes agree. Correct. Answer: 59.

Trap. Dividing 16 by 5 (positions counted instead of gaps) or by 7, which corrupts d before the work even starts. The other slip is treating a_3 = 11 as 'a_1 plus 3d' when it is a_1 plus 2d - working forward from a_7 avoids that step entirely.

Q8 · ARI-SEQ-019 — Answer: C

Step 1 — Quantity A, arithmetic: a_5 = 4 + (5 - 1) x 15 = 4 + 60 = 64. Step 2 — Quantity B, geometric: b_5 = 4 x 2^(5-1) = 4 x 2^4 = 4 x 16 = 64. Step 3 — 64 = 64, so the two quantities are equal. Check by listing. A: 4, 19, 34, 49, 64. B: 4, 8, 16, 32, 64. Both fifth entries are 64. Correct. Note the shape of the two formulas: the arithmetic term adds d exactly (n - 1) times and the geometric term multiplies by r exactly (n - 1) times. Here 4 additions of 15 and 4 doublings happen to land on the same number; one term later the doubling has already run away, 128 against 79. Answer: The two quantities are equal.

Trap. Using the exponent n instead of n - 1 in Quantity B: 4 x 2^5 = 128, which makes Quantity B look greater and gives choice B. The mirror slip, reading the geometric term as first term x n x r = 4 x 5 x 2 = 40, makes Quantity A look greater and gives choice A. The exponent is n - 1 = 4, exactly parallel to the (n - 1)d = 4 x 15 in Quantity A, and with the exponent right the columns tie at 64.

Q9 · ARI-SEQ-021 — Answer: 41

Do not look for a closed form - four terms is faster to list. Step 1 — a_1 = 2. Step 2 — a_2 = 3 x 2 - 1 = 5. Step 3 — a_3 = 3 x 5 - 1 = 14. Step 4 — a_4 = 3 x 14 - 1 = 41. Check by running the rule backwards from 41: (41 + 1)/3 = 14, (14 + 1)/3 = 5, (5 + 1)/3 = 2 = a_1. Correct. Answer: 41.

Trap. Feeding a_1 into the rule for the wrong term, e.g. a_4 = 3 x 2 - 1 = 5, or treating the rule as geometric and computing 2 x 3^3 - 1 = 53. Each term must be built from the one immediately before it; the '-1' is applied at every single step, not once at the end.

Q10 · ARI-SEQ-023 — Answer: A, B, C

The sequence has a = 6 and d = 4, so its terms are 6 + 4k for whole numbers k. A number x is a term exactly when x - 6 is a non-negative multiple of 4. A - 50 - 6 = 44, and 44/4 = 11. True (it is the 12th term). B - 62 - 6 = 56, and 56/4 = 14. True (the 15th term). C - 78 - 6 = 72, and 72/4 = 18. True (the 19th term). D - 100 - 6 = 94, and 94/4 = 23.5, not a whole number. False. E - 200 - 6 = 194, and 194/4 = 48.5, not a whole number. False. Answer: A, B, C.

Trap. Testing only whether the candidate is even. Every term here is even, but the terms are exactly the numbers that leave remainder 2 on division by 4 - and 100 and 200 are multiples of 4, so they are skipped over. The test is (x - a) divisible by d, not (x divisible by d).

Q11 · ARI-SEQ-029 — Answer: B

Step 1 — count the steps between the two given positions: from term 2 to term 5 is 5 - 2 = 3 multiplications by r. Step 2 — so a_5 = a_2 x r^3, giving 162 = 6 x r^3. Step 3 — r^3 = 162/6 = 27, so r = 3. Check by building the sequence: a_2 = 6, a_3 = 18, a_4 = 54, a_5 = 162. Correct. Answer: 3.

Trap. Reporting 27 (choice E), the value of r^3, without taking the cube root. Choice D, 9, is the half-measure of dividing 27 by 3 instead of cube-rooting it. The positions 2 and 5 are three steps apart, not one - the same gap-counting that trips up arithmetic sequences decides the exponent here.

Q12 · ARI-SEQ-034 — Answer: B

Step 2 — count them: (99 - 1)/2 + 1 = 49 + 1 = 50 odd numbers. Step 3 — sum of the first n odd numbers = n^2, so the answer is 50^2 = 2500. Check the long way: 1 + 2 + ... + 100 = 100 x 101/2 = 5050, and 2 + 4 + ... + 100 = 50 x (2 + 100)/2 = 50 x 51 = 2550. Then 5050 - 2550 = 2500. Correct. Answer: 2500.

Trap. Choice A, 2401, is 49^2 - counting only 49 odd numbers by taking (99 - 1)/2 and forgetting the +1. Choice D, 2550, is the even sum on its own, and choice E, 5050, is the total; both come from answering before subtracting. Q13 · ARI-SEQ-039 — Answer: B

Q14 · ARI-SEQ-042 — Answer: C

Step 1 — name the terms symmetrically as a - d, a, a + d. The d's cancel in the sum, which is the whole point of this labeling. Step 2 — sum: (a - d) + a + (a + d) = 3a = 18, so a = 6. In any evenly spaced set the mean equals the middle term. Step 3 — product: (6 - d)(6)(6 + d) = 6(36 - d^2) = 162, so 36 - d^2 = 27, giving d^2 = 9 and d = 3. Step 4 — the numbers are 3, 6, 9. Check: 3 + 6 + 9 = 18 and 3 x 6 x 9 = 162. Correct. Every option already sums to 18, so only the product separates them: A gives 66, B gives 120, D gives 192, E gives 216. Answer: 3, 6, 9.

Trap. All five choices are arithmetic and all five sum to 18, so a student who checks only the sum picks at random. The second trap is choice E: the mean is 6, but 'mean = middle term' does not force all three terms to equal the mean. Only the product 162 pins down d.

Q15 · ARI-SEQ-043 — Answer: 6

Q20 · ARI-SEQ-049 — Answer: A, C, D

First find the rule. The differences are 3, 5, 7, 9 - not constant, so the sequence is not arithmetic. But the differences themselves rise by a constant 2, which signals a quadratic rule. Testing n^2 + 1: 1 + 1 = 2, 4 + 1 = 5, 9 + 1 = 10, 16 + 1 = 17, 25 + 1 = 26. All five match. A - the rule is a_n = n^2 + 1, confirmed on every given term. True. B - a_10 = 10^2 + 1 = 101, not 100. False. C - the differences 3, 5, 7, 9 go up by 2 each time. True. D - a term equals 122 when n^2 + 1 = 122, so n^2 = 121 and n = 11. True. E - a term equals 200 when n^2 = 199, and 199 is not a perfect square (14^2 = 196, 15^2 = 225). False. Answer: A, C, D.

Trap. Choice B is the whole question in miniature: having found the rule n^2 + 1, students compute 10^2 and report 100, dropping the +1 they just derived. Q21 · ARI-SEQ-050 — Answer: D

The insight: chop the sequence into blocks of two. Each block is r^2 times the block before it, because every term is r^2 times the term two places earlier. Step 1 — name the blocks. Block 1 = a_1 + a_2 = 12. Block 2 = a_3 + a_4 = S_4 - S_2 = 36 - 12 = 24. Step 2 — the block ratio: 24/12 = 2, so r^2 = 2. Step 3 — Block 3 = a_5 + a_6 = 24 x r^2 = 24 x 2 = 48. Step 4 — S_6 = S_4 + Block 3 = 36 + 48 = 84. Check by solving outright: r^2 = 2 gives r = sqrt(2), and a(1 + sqrt(2)) = 12 gives a = 12/(1 + sqrt(2)) = 4.9706. The six terms sum to 84.00 to two decimals. Correct. Answer: 84.

Trap. The blocks are 12, 24, 48 - they double. Treating them as an ARITHMETIC progression instead gives a third block of 36 and a total of 72 (choice C), which is the most common wrong answer here. Choice B, 60, repeats the second block unchanged (36 + 24); choice A, 48, adds S_2 to S_4; choice E, 108, uses S_4/S_2 = 3 as the block ratio, forgetting that the second block is S_4 - S_2, not S_4.

Q22 · ARI-SEQ-051 — Answer: C

The amount added changes at every step, so this is not an arithmetic sequence - build it. a_1 = 1 a_2 = 1 + 1 = 2 a_3 = 2 + 2 = 4 a_4 = 4 + 3 = 7 a_5 = 7 + 4 = 11 a_6 = 11 + 5 = 16 a_7 = 16 + 6 = 22 a_8 = 22 + 7 = 29 a_9 = 29 + 8 = 37 a_10 = 37 + 9 = 46 Quantity A = 46 = Quantity B, so the two quantities are equal. Faster route: the increments added on the way to a_10 are 1, 2, 3, ..., 9, so a_10 = 1 + (1 + 2 + ... + 9) = 1 + 9 x 10/2 = 1 + 45 = 46. Correct.

Q23 · ARI-SEQ-054 — Answer: C

With 19 terms the median is the 10th term, so a_10 = 20. Step 3 — walk from the 10th term to the 19th: that is 19 - 10 = 9 steps of 3. a_19 = 20 + 9 x 3 = 20 + 27 = 47. Check: the smallest term is a_1 = 20 - 9 x 3 = -7, and the sum is 19 x (-7 + 47)/2 = 19 x 20 = 380. Correct. Answer: 47.

Q24 · ARI-SEQ-056 — Answer: -5050

Step 1 — pair the terms in the order they are written: (1^2 - 2^2) + (3^2 - 4^2) + ... + (99^2 - 100^2). There are 100 terms, so exactly 50 pairs. Step 2 — factor each pair as a difference of squares: k^2 - (k+1)^2 = (k - (k+1))(k + (k+1)) = -(2k + 1). So each pair is simply minus the sum of its two bases: 1^2 - 2^2 = -3, 3^2 - 4^2 = -7, 5^2 - 6^2 = -11, and so on. Step 3 — the pair values are -3, -7, -11, ..., -199, an arithmetic list with d = -4. Count: (199 - 3)/4 + 1 = 49 + 1 = 50 pairs, matching Step 1. Step 4 — sum them: 50 x (-3 + -199)/2 = 50 x (-101) = -5050. Check by a second route: since each pair equals -(sum of its two bases), the whole expression equals -(1 + 2 + 3 + ... + 100) = -(100 x 101/2) = -5050. Correct. Answer: -5050.

Trap. Computing 100 squares by hand, which is a guaranteed arithmetic error inside a two-minute question. The conceptual traps are dropping the minus sign and answering 5050, and mis-pairing as 1^2 - (2^2 + 3^2) - ... by ignoring that the signs alternate strictly term by term.

Q25 · ARI-SEQ-057 — Answer: A, C, E

So the values a_n - 1 form a geometric sequence with first term a_1 - 1 = 1 and ratio 3, which means a_n = 3^(n-1) + 1. Step 3 - a number is a term exactly when it is 1 more than a power of 3 (1, 3, 9, 27, 81, 243, 729, ...). A: 28 = 27 + 1. True. B: 81 = 80 + 1, and 80 is not a power of 3. False (81 is itself a power of 3, which is the trap). C: 82 = 81 + 1. True. D: 243 = 242 + 1, and 242 is not a power of 3. False. E: 730 = 729 + 1. True. Answer: A, C, E.

Trap. Students notice powers of 3 and select 81 and 243 (B and D). The terms are one more than a power of 3, so 28, 82 and 730 qualify instead.

Q26 · ARI-SEQ-061 — Answer: C

Step 1 — Quantity A, count the multiples of 3 from 3 to 300. They are 3 x 1, 3 x 2, ..., 3 x 100, so there are 100 of them; the spacing formula agrees: (300 - 3)/3 + 1 = 99 + 1 = 100. Step 2 — strip out the ones that are also multiples of 5, that is the multiples of 15: 15, 30, ..., 300, and (300 - 15)/15 + 1 = 19 + 1 = 20 of them. Step 3 — Quantity A = 100 - 20 = 80. Step 4 — Quantity B, count the integers from 50 to 150: 150 - 50 + 1 = 101. Step 5 — strip out the multiples of 5 in that range: 50, 55, ..., 150, and (150 - 50)/5 + 1 = 20 + 1 = 21 of them. Step 6 — Quantity B = 101 - 21 = 80. 80 = 80, so the two quantities are equal. Check: all four counts are (last - first)/step + 1, and each one needs its own +1. Drop a single one of those four +1s and the tie disappears, which is exactly what the wrong answers are made of. Answer: The two quantities are equal.

Trap. Dropping the +1 on the total in Quantity B, 150 - 50 = 100, gives 100 - 21 = 79 and makes Quantity A look greater, choice A. Dropping the +1 on the multiples of 5 instead, (150 - 50)/5 = 20, gives 101 - 20 = 81 and makes Quantity B look greater, choice B - the same off-by-one lands on either wrong letter depending on which of the four counts it hits. Forgetting to remove the multiples of 15 from Quantity A leaves 100 against 80 and also points at A.

Q27 · SEC-AO-010 — Answer: C

Each term is one third of the previous one, so Z(i) = 3 x (1/3)^(i - 1) = 3^(2 - i). Quantity A is therefore Z(5) = 3^(2 - 5) = 3^(-3) = 1/27. For Quantity B, Z(13) = 3^(2 - 13) = 3^(-11), and 9^4 = (3^2)^4 = 3^8, so Quantity B = 3^8 x 3^(-11) = 3^(-3) = 1/27. The two quantities are equal.

Trap. Students see an early term compared against a much later one in a decreasing sequence and assume Quantity A must win, without converting 9^4 into 3^8 to notice it cancels the eight extra divisions exactly. Rewriting both sides on a common base is what collapses the comparison.

Q28 · SEC-AO-027 — Answer: D

The two points differ by 24 in the x-direction and by 3 in the y-direction, so they are the legs of a right triangle with the distance as its hypotenuse. By the distance formula, a(n) = sqrt((n - 0)^2 + (0 - 3)^2) = sqrt(n^2 + 9). Substituting n = 24 gives a(24) = sqrt(24^2 + 9) = sqrt(576 + 9) = sqrt(585).

Trap. Students subtract the squares instead of adding them, treating 24 as the hypotenuse, and answer sqrt(567). Others add the coordinates directly to get 24 + 3 = 27 and choose sqrt(27). The point (0, 3) sits on the y-axis and (24, 0) on the x-axis, so both coordinate gaps are legs and both squares must be added.

Q29 · ARI-SEQ-059 — Answer: D

Step 1 — write the ratio. S_n = (n/2)[2a + (n-1)d], so Quantity A = [n(2a + (2n-1)d)] / [(n/2)(2a + (n-1)d)] = 2(2a + (2n-1)d)/(2a + (n-1)d). The n does not cancel out of the brackets, so the ratio depends on a, d and n together. Test the extremes. Step 2 — make d small next to a. Take a = 1, d = 1, n = 1: S_2 = 1 + 2 = 3 and S_1 = 1, so the ratio is 3, which is LESS than 4. Step 3 — make d large next to a. Take a = 1, d = 100, n = 1: S_2 = 1 + 101 = 102 and S_1 = 1, so the ratio is 102, which is greater than 4. Both cases satisfy every condition in the stem, and they land on opposite sides of 4. The relationship cannot be determined. A third data point: a = 1, d = 1, n = 5 gives S_10/S_5 = 55/15 = 11/3, again below 4.

Trap. Assuming that doubling the number of terms multiplies the sum by a fixed factor - by 2 (as it would if all terms were equal) or by 4 (as it would if a = 0, where S_n is proportional to n^2). Both are special cases, not the general rule: as d/a grows the ratio climbs without limit, and as d/a shrinks toward 0 it falls toward 2. Anything above 2 is reachable, so no single comparison with 4 holds.

Q30 · ARI-SEQ-KMF-002 — Answer: C
